# Chapter One — *The Lord of Hexani*
## Part 1 of 3

---

The shuttle came down through low cloud and a wash of mining smoke, and Jaina Solo Fel watched the world below resolve into something older than its file said it was.

Hexani V on the Alliance charts read *low-yield rock, two settlements, one orbital refinery, decommissioned*. The charts were ten years out of date. The world that climbed up to meet them now had three orbital rings, a crawl of yard-cradles around the larger one, and a city that had spilled out of its original walls and kept spilling, southward along a river that ran the colour of weak caf. The hills behind the city were stitched with cuts where the old mines had been; the cuts had been planted over and the planting had taken in patches, so the slopes looked half-shaved, half-furred, like an animal mid-moult.

“They’ve been busy,” Senator G’Mai T’oni said.

The senator was a small woman with violet montrals and a steady, tired voice. She had been the only member of the standing committee willing to fly out personally, which Jaina took to mean either that she was the only senator with nerve or that she was the only one Cal Omas could spare. Probably both. T’oni stood at the viewport with both hands on the rail and her shoulders set as if the planet might rear up under her if she leaned the wrong way.

“They’ve been busy for a while,” Jaina said.

She kept her own voice level. The Force had been quiet across the descent — not the false quiet of a null field, not yet, just the wide hush of a planet that hadn’t been a player long enough to have a song the way Coruscant or Bastion or Kashyyyk did. Hexani V was new to itself. That made it harder to read.

Kyp Durron lowered himself onto the bench across the aisle and laced his fingers between his knees. He had spent the last forty minutes pacing the cabin in the careful, rationed way of a man who knew pacing wouldn’t help and was doing it anyway. Now he looked at the planet through Jaina’s window and made a low sound in his throat.

“Look at the towers,” he said.

There were two, one at either end of the city, where the old Imperial garrison plans would have placed a single command spike. Both of them stood finished. Both of them flew banners.

“He’s building,” Jag said.

Jagged Fel had not moved from his seat since they cleared the upper atmosphere. He sat with his right hand on his knee and his left wrist resting on the arm of the chair, the way a man sits when he is determined to look at ease and is not at ease. His scar caught the cabin lights. He had read the Imperial Remnant intelligence file twice on the flight in and once more during the planetary approach, and he had not asked Jaina what she thought of any of it, which Jaina had taken to mean that he was waiting for her to ask him.

She did not, yet. She would. Later. When they were alone.

“Banners,” Kyp said. “Not Imperial.”

“No,” Jag said. “His own.”

Jaina narrowed her eyes against the glare. From this height the cloth was just colour — a deep red, almost black, with a slash of dull bronze through it. No cog. No starbird. No double-headed Krayt. Whatever device the banner carried, it wasn’t one of the houses she had been raised to recognise, and that, on its own, was telling. Lord-warlords in the Outer Rim usually pirated their iconography from somewhere. They wore borrowed glory because they didn’t have their own yet. This one had decided not to bother.

“What is it?” T’oni asked.

“A flag,” Kyp said.

T’oni did not look at him. “I can see it’s a flag, Master Durron.”

“It’s a wound,” Jaina said.

She heard the senator turn her head. She didn’t look back.

“The colour,” Jaina said. “That’s old Hexani slag. The slope colour from the cuts. The bronze is the tibanna-burn from the refineries — what gets left in the catch pans when you cook the gas off the wrong way. They’re wearing what was done to the planet.”

Jag said, without inflection, “That is either very clever or very cynical.”

“Yes,” Jaina said.

“Both is possible,” Jag said.

“Yes,” Jaina said again.

The shuttle banked. Below them, a column of grey-painted speeders moved through one of the lower districts in column-of-twos, escorting what looked like a civilian water-tanker. The tanker had a green stripe down its side. Jaina knew without being told that the green stripe was the Hexani mark for *humanitarian, do not target*, because the briefing had said so, and because the brief had also said that the warlord was unusual in that he actually honoured the stripe.

Honoured it for his own. He was less particular about whose tanker it had been before he took it.

“Three minutes,” the pilot called back.

T’oni straightened her robe. It was a deep grey, narrowly cut, almost military, and Jaina noted again that the senator had chosen not to dress like a senator — no white, no court collar, no rank-pin. T’oni had read the same brief Jaina had and had decided, evidently, that walking into Hexani Prime in Alliance whites would look like a man wearing his bantha-leather shoes to a slaughter yard. Sensible woman.

Kyp had made the opposite choice. He wore his cloak open over plain dark robes and his lightsaber clipped where it could be seen. Jaina had not argued with him about it. There were arguments worth having with Kyp Durron and there were arguments that were just a way of getting tired before a fight.

Jag wore Fel black. Of course he did.

“Master Solo,” T’oni said, very quietly.

“Master Solo Fel,” Jaina said. It came out automatically. She corrected herself a beat after. “Sorry. Either.”

“You answer to both?”

“I answer to either.”

“He’ll use the one that annoys you,” T’oni said.

Jaina looked at the senator. T’oni looked back, dry, without smiling, and Jaina remembered for a small clear moment that Cal Omas had not sent a fool.

“Probably,” Jaina said.

“Then he wins that one,” T’oni said, “and we save the win for somewhere it matters. Don’t correct him.”

“Noted.”

“He may also,” T’oni said, lower still, “lie. Frequently. Casually. About things that don’t matter. The reports say he does it the way other men chew.”

“Noted.”

T’oni nodded once and turned back to the viewport.

The shuttle dropped through the last bank of cloud and the city came up around them — not under, around, because the landing pad they were aimed at sat halfway up the wall of a worked-out canyon, with the original mine-cut visible above as a sheared cliff of grey-black stone and the city visible below as a stagger of roofs going down to the river. There were lights on, even though it was full day. They were burning hot enough to be needed.

“Tibanna gas-light,” Jag said. He had been studying the same view. “They’re cooking their own at the source and piping it up.”

“Cheap,” Kyp said.

“Cheaper than they had,” Jag said, “before he was here.”

Jaina watched the pad approach. There were figures on it already, drawn up in two short ranks, white against the dark of the apron. The whites were not quite white. She’d known that from the brief; she saw it now and her chest tightened anyway, in the small reflexive way that white armour did, that the order she had grown up with had spent eighteen years teaching her to read as *threat*.

“Two ranks,” Jag said.

“Eleven each,” Kyp said.

“One short on the left,” Jag said.

“Honour squad,” Kyp said. “He’s sending his honour squad and one of them just got pulled. That’s sloppy, or it’s a message.”

“Or it’s real,” Jaina said. “Some of them are still soldiers, Kyp.”

Kyp did not answer. He looked at her with the steady half-tilt of someone who had wanted to answer and had decided not to and would be telling her later that he had not.

The shuttle settled. Hydraulics hissed. The pilot called, “Locked,” and somewhere aft the ramp began its slow drop down to the duracrete.

Senator T’oni took a breath, let it out, and laid her hand briefly on Jaina’s sleeve.

“Master Solo,” T’oni said. “Walk in like we’re here to listen. He’ll find out the rest on his own.”

Jaina nodded.

She let her hand rest, just for a half second, on the hilt of her lightsaber. Not for show. To find herself. To find the weight of the thing in the place where it sat. Then she let her hand fall and walked down the ramp at the senator’s shoulder.

The wind off the slopes carried the smell of cooked rock and old smoke and something faintly floral she could not place. The shadow of the canyon wall lay over half the pad. The other half burned bright in the cold high sun. The two ranks of troopers stood inside the shadow.

They were not standing the way she had expected.

She had expected the old Imperial parade brace — heels together, spine planked, eyes fixed at infinity, the kind of posture you took when your training sergeant had spent a year making sure no part of you moved without his say-so. These men weren’t standing like that. They were at a kind of half-rest, weight even, weapons across the chest in a relaxed sling-port that put the muzzle clear of any line through the delegation but kept the rifle in immediate use. It was a working stance. They looked like they had been on duty a long time and were good at being on duty and weren’t pretending otherwise.

The armour was different too.

It was Imperial-pattern. Anyone who had grown up the way Jaina had grown up could see that at a glance — the helmet line, the shoulder bell, the chest plate cut. But the surface had been treated. Where the old white had been smooth and bright and brittle, this had been hazed down with a dull grey-green wash and then matte-coated over the top, so the plates didn’t throw the sun the way Imperial armour was supposed to. They weren’t troopers as a symbol any more. They were troopers as a tool.

And on the back of every one of them, between the shoulder blades, rode a small flat unit the size of a folded jacket. A short coil ran from the unit up into the collar housing. A heat strip along the bottom edge glowed a soft amber.

“Shield generators,” Kyp murmured.

“On every one,” Jag said.

It was not in either of their voices but Jaina could hear them thinking the same thing she was. Personal shielding on every common trooper was Mandalorian-clan luxury. It was Royal Guard kit. It was Sun Guard kit. It was not what you put on a man you were planning to spend.

The trooper at the right of the front rank stepped forward one pace and lifted his helmet off in a single clean movement, the way a man took his hat off in a building. He was a sergeant, by the slash of bronze paint on his pauldron. He had a broad pale face and a recent shave and eyes that did the careful read across the four of them — Senator, Imperial Head of State, Jedi Master, Jedi Master — without lingering on any one.

“Senator,” the sergeant said. He nodded to T’oni first, which was correct. Then, “Master Solo Fel.” He nodded to Jaina, which was also correct, and used the longer name without any apparent edge to it. Then to Jag: “Your Majesty.” He paused on Kyp. “Master Durron.”

Kyp said, “Sergeant.”

“Honour to escort,” the sergeant said. His voice was flat. He was reciting from a brief and he didn’t pretend he wasn’t. “His Lordship’s compliments. He asks I bring you in by the south colonnade. The lift to the audience floor is in the keep proper. Refreshments laid on. The walk is six minutes. He apologises for the walk.”

“Does he,” Jag said.

“He does, Your Majesty,” the sergeant said. Nothing moved in his face. “He says he’d send a speeder but the south colonnade is the part of the city that worked first, and he wants you to see it.”

T’oni gave the sergeant a small precise smile. “We’ll walk.”

“Ma’am.”

The sergeant turned. The two ranks did not quite snap; they shifted, deliberate, professional, and re-formed as a corridor in the same movement. As they did, the trooper at the left end of the second rank moved a half step inward and Jaina got her first clear look at what he was carrying.

It was not a rifle.

It was a frame, slung across his chest the way a heavy comm-pack would have been, and it ended in a short blunt emitter the size of a clenched fist. A bank of indicator lights ran along the top edge of the unit. A coolant line ran from the side of it back over the trooper’s shoulder and disappeared into a second pack at his hip. The whole thing was painted in the same dull grey-green as the armour, but the bronze paint of the unit number had been touched onto the side: a single digit, and a slash.

She did not need Kyp to tell her. She did not need to ask.

The Force around the trooper was — wrong was not the word, exactly. The Force was still there. It was still under her hand. It was the same Force she had carried since she was a child, the same hum she had walked with into Borleias and out of Coruscant and across half the galaxy. But something about the air a metre out from that trooper was rolled, and curdled, and would not settle. When she let her attention brush the corridor of his carry, the brush came back with a small wrong sound. Not silence. Lie.

She made her face quiet.

Beside her, Kyp had not made his face quiet. Kyp had stopped two steps shorter than he should have, and his shoulders had come up under his cloak, and his right hand had risen halfway to his belt before he caught it and stopped it and let it down.

“Master Durron,” the sergeant said. He had not turned around. He had felt the pause behind him — or, more probably, the trooper at the left end had keyed it into his earpiece. “If it’s easier we can route you wide of Trooper Vesh.”

“No,” Kyp said. His jaw worked once. “No. It’s fine.”

“Sir.”

The sergeant began to walk. T’oni went after him. Jag fell in at T’oni’s right shoulder; Jaina took her left; Kyp brought up behind, and Jaina did not have to look back to know that Kyp was placing himself, deliberately, on the side of the corridor that put the null-array trooper at his eye.

They went into the south colonnade and the planet closed around them.

---

The colonnade was an old Imperial works gallery — Jaina knew the bones of it from the same childhood that had taught her to read armour. It had been built to walk a sector governor from a landing pad to a keep without making him stand in weather. The columns were the standard squared durasteel, painted the standard Imperial grey, and somebody had at some point come along and painted a band of dull bronze around the base of every column at knee height, so that the line of them ran down the length of the gallery like a low fence.

The bronze was not perfect. In places it had been laid over older paint — a smear of black that read, when she stepped close, as the remains of a stencil. A starbird, half-scraped. Somebody had painted Rebel marks on these columns once. Somebody else had painted Imperial back over them. Somebody else had hazed both layers down and put the bronze line on top.

“They didn’t clean it,” Jag said, quietly.

“No,” Jaina said.

“The Empire would have.”

“Yes.”

“He hasn’t.”

“No.”

Jag was silent for two paces.

“He wants us to see it,” Jag said.

“Yes.”

“Which means he wants us to think a particular thing about him.”

“Yes.”

“Which means we should think more than that thing,” Jag said.

She let the corner of her mouth move. Just barely.

“You are,” she said, “tremendous fun at parties.”

“This is not a party, Empress.”

“Don’t call me that.”

“On the record.”

“Don’t call me that on the record either.”

He didn’t answer. He was looking, again, at the columns, and at the troopers spaced down them at six-pace intervals, and at the small flat units rising on every back between every set of shoulder blades.

They were not in formation, the troopers along the gallery. They were on post. They had the easy, slightly bored standing-down of men who had been told who was coming and that the people coming were not, today, the enemy. They turned their helmets — slightly, just the visor — as the delegation passed. They did not present arms. The sergeant had not asked them to. Apparently, on Hexani V, a delegation walked past a man on post without ceremony, and the ceremony was the absence of it. Another deliberate thing.

Jaina counted as she walked.

Two troopers per six paces. Each carrying the personal shield. Two of them, every twelfth pace or so, with the heavier pack she now knew to call the squad automatic — a thing she had seen in the brief as a DLT-19 variant with an upgraded sight cradle, a weapon for laying down a base of fire while other men moved. One trooper per platoon, the brief had said, would carry the null array. She counted three before they were halfway down the colonnade and she stopped counting because she didn’t like the answer.

“They aren’t showing us the cheap stuff,” Kyp said, low.

“No,” Jaina said.

“The kit, on every man.”

“Yes.”

“Even the gate guard.”

“Yes.”

“Either he can field every man in the army like this — ”

“Or this is the army he wants us to count,” Jaina said.

“Yes.”

“Yes,” she said.

They went on.

Halfway down the gallery, the columns broke open on the left to show the south face of the city. The face fell away in terraces of pale brick down to the river. Lifts ran up the terrace walls, and people were on them — a lot of people, a flow of them, going up and down, more than the brief had implied. The brief had implied a depressed mining town in the slow process of being made a capital by an upstart. The view said a working city. Cranes turned over a half-built medcentre at the second terrace down. A row of grain silos at the river had been re-roofed in clean new sheet, and the sheet still flashed where the protective film hadn’t been peeled. Past the silos, a market square had a queue at one corner — not a bread queue, Jaina realised after a beat; the queue was at a registry booth. Three desks under a canopy. People were signing in.

“What are they signing?” T’oni said.

The sergeant slowed without turning his head.

“Hab assignments, Senator,” he said. “New blocks coming online south of the river. His Lordship moved sixteen hundred families out of the old refinery quarter when we opened the new lines. They’re drawing keys this week.”

“Out of the refinery quarter,” T’oni said.

“Yes ma’am.”

“To where, exactly.”

“Forks Hill, ma’am. New build. Two-room and three-room. Plumbed. Lighting. School site on the corner.”

“And the refinery quarter is being —”

“Levelled, ma’am.”

T’oni waited.

“For another medcentre, ma’am,” the sergeant said. “The chief surgeon’s been asking for one for a year. His Lordship’s been promising. His Lordship is making good on it. That’s the line, ma’am, if you’d like the line.”

T’oni said, “Thank you, Sergeant.”

She didn’t look at Jaina. She didn’t need to. They had both heard the *the line*.

Jag had heard it too. Jag had heard everything. Jag did not move his head; he was a man who could listen with a quarter-millimetre of his face, and a quarter-millimetre of his face was listening now.

Kyp said, behind them, “Sixteen hundred families.”

“Yes, sir,” the sergeant said.

“And the ones who didn’t want to move?”

The sergeant did not slow this time.

“They moved, sir.”

Kyp opened his mouth.

“Master Durron,” Jaina said. Quietly. Not turning.

Kyp closed his mouth.

They walked on.

The colonnade ended at a set of double doors that had once been Imperial — the cog-and-bar over the lintel was the give-away — and had been re-cut around the cog so the cog had been left in place and a slot run around it. A new piece of bronze had been set into the slot, beaten thin, hammered into the shape of the slag-and-burn device they had seen on the banner from the air. The old cog showed through the middle of it like an eye.

“He left the cog,” T’oni said.

“Yes, ma’am.”

“Why.”

“He says it was already there, ma’am.”

“That is not, by itself, a reason.”

“No, ma’am.”

“Sergeant,” T’oni said, “does he give you the *reason* often?”

The sergeant looked at her, finally. He had the careful, weighted patience that Jaina had seen in line sergeants from a dozen different services. He was not insulted by the question. He had simply heard versions of it before.

“He gives me my orders, ma’am,” he said. “The reasons are his.”

“And the reasons satisfy you.”

“The orders are good ones, ma’am,” the sergeant said. “I don’t go past that.”

He turned back to the doors.

The doors opened.

---

The lift was an honest Imperial keep lift, the kind that grumbled, and they rode up in silence with two troopers behind them and the null-array trooper somewhere on the lower stair. Jaina could still feel him. Eight metres down, around a turn, behind plating: she could still feel him, the way you could still feel a low note buzzing inside the panel of a speeder even after the engine was off. The Force didn’t go silent around him. The Force went *unreliable* around him. She found herself, for the second time in five minutes, having to deliberately stop reaching toward the trooper to test it. It was the kind of itch you could not scratch without making worse.

Kyp had a grey cast across his cheekbones. Kyp was trying not to scratch the itch too.

“Master Solo,” T’oni murmured. She was looking at the lift wall and not at Jaina. “You said it isn’t a null bubble.”

“No,” Jaina said.

“What is it.”

“He’s — ” She put the words together carefully, because she wanted to put them down well, once, and then not have to revisit them. “The Force is still there, Senator. It’s like… it’s like reading by a lamp that flickers. The light’s still light. You can read by it. You can’t trust what you’re reading.”

T’oni did not look at her.

“Will it be on,” T’oni said, “in the room.”

“I don’t know,” Jaina said.

“Master Durron?”

Kyp said, very low, “It will be on in the room.”

“Mm,” T’oni said. She faced front. The lift kept climbing.

Jag put his hand on Jaina’s back. Briefly. Lightly. The flat of his palm just under her shoulder blade. He did not say anything. She did not turn her head. She let it be there for the count of three and then he took it away again.

She didn’t need it for steadiness. She had her steadiness. She took it as it was meant — as a present-tense thing, a here-with-you thing, the small private signal that Jagged Fel had been making to her, with one hand, in public, for almost as long as they had been married. *I see what you see. I am here.*

The lift stopped.

The doors split open and the audience floor of the Hexani keep opened in front of them, and Jaina got her first surprise of the day.

She had been expecting a throne room. The brief had used the word *audience*; *audience* meant a high room with a long approach, a man at the far end, distance for him to be looked at across. She had been ready for that. She had been ready, even, to be amused by it — to let Jag and Kyp do the wary thing while she let her shoulders down and didn’t play the man’s pageant.

The room they walked into was not a throne room.

It was a working room. A wide, square chamber, ceilings high enough to be old Imperial, floor still showing the chequer of the original deck-tile under a layer of newer mat. Three long tables had been pushed end-to-end down the middle, with chairs on either side; the chairs did not match. A topographical hologram stood on a plinth in the far corner, rotating slowly through what looked like a frontier sector — Jaina recognised, at one corner of it, the spiral of the Braxant Run. A galley nook in the far right wall had a samovar going, and a young woman in a plain grey tunic was pouring out cups from it onto a tray. The far left wall was a working comm board, currently dimmed. The ceiling, where the Empire would have hung a banner, was bare.

There were no guards in the room.

There were no guards at the door inside the room. The two troopers who had ridden up in the lift with them had stopped at the threshold and stayed in the lobby outside. The young woman at the samovar was unarmed. The two staff officers standing at the topographical plinth — one in a kind of grey field uniform with a single bronze pip at the collar, the other a thin older man in shirtsleeves with a stylus tucked behind his ear — had not even turned around as the lift doors opened.

And there was, behind the head of the long table, a man.

He was on his feet. He had clearly been on his feet for a while. He had a flimsi in his left hand and a half-eaten piece of flatbread in his right, and he was reading the flimsi while chewing the flatbread, and one bare foot was hooked behind the calf of the other in the small unselfconscious shift of a man who had been standing too long and was trying to get the cramp out of his leg without sitting down.

He was not in robes. He was not in armour. He was not in anything with rank on it. He was in dark working trousers, a grey crew-neck undershirt, and a heavier short coat thrown over the shirt and not buttoned. The coat had a small bronze pin at the lapel — a slash, like the slash on the banner. That was it. That was the whole of the costume of the Lord of Hexani.

He looked up.

“Oh,” he said. He said it around the flatbread. He swallowed, fast, awkwardly, the way a man does when he has to swallow before he can be polite. “Oh. Right. Hi.”

He set the flimsi down. He set the flatbread, more carefully, on top of the flimsi, the way a man puts down a thing he is going to come back to. He wiped his hand on the side of his trousers. He came round the head of the table.

Jaina’s first read on Jiyad Maerko was a kind of small flat surprise, which she would, later, be annoyed at herself for.

He was not tall. The briefs had said *not tall*, and the briefs had not lied, but the briefs had also implied a wiry build, a desert build, the lean fighter look that holos liked, and Maerko was not that either. He was a heavy man — built thick across the shoulder and the back and the upper arm in the deliberate, slow way of a man who picked things up off the floor in his free time because he liked picking things up off the floor. The neck was a working neck. The forearms were the forearms of someone who did not have to fake them. He was not fat. He was not graceful. He was, as the brief had said, *easily forgettable on a corner*, except that on a corner you would have noticed how solid he was and then walked on.

His face was — the brief had also been accurate about this. There were no scars. There was nothing dramatic. His hair was cropped short and starting to recede at the temples in the way that men’s hair did in their early thirties when the early thirties had been busy. He hadn’t shaved in a day. The mouth was set in a small fixed frown, like the brief had said — like he was trying to swallow a lemon, the brief had said, and Jaina caught, before she could stop herself, a snort behind her teeth, because the brief had been right about that too. It really did look exactly like that.

His eyes were the give-away.

His eyes were dark and quiet and the quiet was a kind that came from doing a lot of things you couldn’t take back. Jaina knew that quiet. She had grown up around it. She had married into it. She had a brother who had, for a season, worn that quiet on his face like a man wears the wrong coat in winter.

Maerko stopped two paces short of the table’s end and put his weight evenly on both feet and looked at the four of them, briefly, the way a working sergeant looks at a draft showing up to his platoon. Then he held out his hand. Right hand. Palm dry, no glove, the working callouses on the knuckles plain.

“Senator,” he said. “G’Mai T’oni, yeah?”

“Yes,” T’oni said. She took his hand.

“Got that right?” He squeezed once, let go. “*Gah-MY*. Long *I*. Sorry. I’m crap at names. Tell me if I mangle it later.”

“You said it correctly,” T’oni said.

“Good. Right.” He turned. “Your Majesty.” He took Jag’s hand. He did not bow. Jag did not bow either. Two heads of state shaking on a Tuesday. “Thanks for coming personally. I didn’t expect that.”

“I prefer to see a thing,” Jag said.

“Yeah.” Maerko gave him a small, almost amused, almost not, almost private look. “Me too.”

He turned. “Master Durron.” He took Kyp’s hand. The handshake was slightly slower. Maerko did not break the eye contact. Kyp did not break the eye contact. It went on a quarter of a second longer than it had any business going on, and then Maerko let go and turned to Jaina.

He looked at her.

He looked at her, properly, with both eyes and his whole face, for the first time since they had come into the room — and Jaina, who had not opened her sense outward since the lift, had not even brushed at it because of the trooper, felt his attention land on her with the small dry thump of a thrown coat hitting a hook.

“Master Solo Fel,” he said.

“Master Solo,” she said. “Either.”

“No,” he said. “I’ll do the both. Your father did the both at Endor. Your father is the only Solo I’ve ever had a beer with. I’m gonna do the both.”

Jaina’s mouth opened.

“My father,” she said, carefully, “had a beer with you.”

“Couple of years ago,” Maerko said. He had not let go of her hand yet. He was not gripping it. He was just standing there with his palm against her palm and his eyes on her eyes. “Outer Rim run. I was — I was nobody yet. Smuggler’s bar. Nar Shaddaa. He didn’t know who I was. I knew who he was. Bought him the second round. He told me a story about a Bothan with a parrot. I’m not gonna repeat the story, ma’am, no offence.”

“None taken,” Jaina said.

He let her hand go.

“I’m Jiyad Maerko,” he said.

“I know,” she said.

“Yeah,” he said. He gave a small, dry, almost rueful breath through his nose. “Yeah, I figured.”

Then his face did the thing.

She would, later, not be able to describe it well to Jag. It wasn’t a smile. It wasn’t a smirk. It wasn’t any of the practised things a public man learned how to do with the lower half of his face. What it was was a quick small *settling*, a thing falling into place behind the eyes, as if Maerko had been carrying around a particular weight all morning and the weight had now found its peg. His shoulders did not move. His mouth did not move. The frown stayed exactly the lemon frown the brief had described. But something in him fitted, briefly, into the air, and Jaina — who had not been open to him on purpose — caught the edge of it anyway, because the Force in the room was sticky enough that she could not have closed it down on its own without working at it.

What she caught was not malice.

It wasn’t friendship either.

It was *Right, then. Now we can start.*

Maerko stepped back. He flicked a hand at the table.

“Sit,” he said. “Please. Senator, end. Master Solo Fel, my right. Your Majesty, my left. Master Durron, anywhere you like, I don’t mind. Sergeant —” He raised his voice slightly without raising it to anything you’d call loud. “Sergeant, that’ll do, thank you. Karet, tea, please. Senator takes hers no sugar, the Master takes hers with, His Majesty takes his off the leaf at the second pour. Master Durron —” He looked at Kyp. “What do you drink?”

“Water,” Kyp said.

“Water,” Maerko said.

“Just water.”

Maerko stood, looking at him, for the count of one.

“Karet,” he said, without turning. “Water for Master Durron. Cold. Glass jug. Cut lemon if we’ve got it. Don’t we’ve got it. Forget the lemon. Sorry, Master Durron, we’ve had a run on lemon.”

“It’s fine.”

“It’s not fine but we’ll live.” Maerko turned back to the table and pulled out the chair at its head with one hand and dropped into it the way a working man drops onto a working chair, without ceremony, the back of the chair complaining and the leg of it kicking out a little under his weight. He hooked one boot over the bar of the chair opposite. He laid both forearms on the table. He looked, for an instant, less like a warlord and more like a foreman who had called a contractors’ meeting on a Friday afternoon.

“Right,” he said. “Sit down. Please.”

They sat down.

He let the silence run for a second. He was, Jaina realised, letting them all settle into the chairs and the room and the smell of the tea and the slight wrong hum of the air, because the slight wrong hum of the air was very much *on* — she could feel it now, here, in the room with him, low and constant, a half-key flat in the song of the place. The null array — or, more likely, a small built-in one somewhere in the walls — was running, and Maerko had not mentioned it, and was not going to mention it, and was waiting, in fact, to see if any of them mentioned it first.

T’oni did not.

Jag did not.

Kyp set his hands flat on the table and breathed out through his nose, once, and did not.

Jaina did not.

Maerko’s mouth twitched. Not a smile. A small private acknowledgement.

“Okay,” he said. “Good.”

“Good?” T’oni said.

“Good we’re past it.”

T’oni waited.

“The hum,” Maerko said. “I’ve got a — yeah. It’s on. Low setting. I’ll turn it down if it itches anybody. I won’t turn it off. We’ll get further with it on. Trust me on that one or we can leave now, your choice.”

T’oni said, after a beat, “Leave it on, Lord Maerko.”

“Lord’s fine,” Maerko said. He grimaced, briefly. “Lord is — I picked it because it’s the shortest. *Your Eminence*, I’d shoot myself. *Your Highness*, I’d shoot somebody else. *Lord* is short. I don’t love it. It’ll do.”

T’oni almost smiled. She did not let it on her face. Jaina saw it move under the surface anyway.

“Lord Maerko,” T’oni said.

“Senator T’oni.”

“Shall we begin.”

“Yeah,” Maerko said. He sat back. He laid one heavy forearm over the chair back. He looked at her across the length of the table. “Yeah, let’s. Tell me what the Alliance wants.”

T’oni opened her folder.

Maerko held up the flat of his hand.

“No,” he said. “I don’t mean the brief. I’ve read the brief. Your office sent it three days ahead, which I appreciate, that was nice of you, somebody on your staff has manners. I don’t want the brief. I want what the Alliance *wants*, Senator. Off the script. From you. Right now. One sentence. Don’t give me ten.”

T’oni closed the folder.

She was, Jaina noted, very good at this. T’oni had not even glanced down to mark her place in the folder before she closed it. She knew her brief well enough not to need it.

“We want you,” T’oni said, “to stop.”

“Stop what.”

“Taking systems.”

Maerko looked at her. He did not move. He did not blink.

“Right,” he said. “No.”

He said it the way a man says *no* to a second helping of stew. He had said it before; he would say it again; it was a *no* without heat. He did not lean forward. He did not raise his voice. He looked at the senator across the table and said *no* and the *no* did not need to be defended.

T’oni waited.

Maerko waited.

It was T’oni who moved first. She nodded once, slow, as if filing the *no* away in a drawer she had brought with her for the purpose.

“All right,” she said. “Then what do *you* want, Lord Maerko.”

“Right,” he said. He rocked once on the back legs of his chair. The chair complained again. “Right. Let’s have it.” He laid both forearms back on the table and looked, briefly, at each of them in turn — Jag, Jaina, Kyp, T’oni — and made the same small *settling* he had made when he had first taken Jaina’s hand. The frown did not move. Something behind it did.

“What I want,” he said, “is the Braxant Run. Wide. From the Mygeeto turn all the way out to Bastion approach. Free passage for my carriers and for any registered Hexani hull. Toll-free. Inspection-light. I don’t want to *own* it. I want it *open*. Same for the Celanon Spur, end to end. Same for the eastern leg of the Perlemian, from Brentaal IV out to the Ploo intersection. I want my freighters to be able to take a hot Eastern lane out of my space and into anybody’s without getting boarded by an Alliance lieutenant who needs the bonus.”

“And the seventeen systems —”

“Mine,” Maerko said.

“Lord Maerko —”

“Mine, Senator.”

“There are populated worlds in those —”

“They’re *mine*, Senator. They voted, in the eight of them where there was a thing to vote on. In the other nine — yeah, fine. Sword. Whatever. I’m not giving them back. I’m feeding them. I’m feeding them, which is more than the Alliance was doing when I took them. Hexani Forks Hill medcentre? That is in a system the brief calls *contested*. It’s not contested. It’s under build. Don’t come at me on the worlds.”

“The Confederation,” T’oni said, evenly, “is asking the Galactic Alliance to legitimise the conquest of nine populated systems.”

“The Confederation,” Maerko said, evenly, “is asking the Galactic Alliance to *get the hell out of my way* on nine systems I already have, and to let me move a freighter from one side of my house to the other without your customs people taking a cut.”

“Those are not the same ask.”

“They’re the same ask, Senator. They’re the same ask with different paint.”

“And in exchange.”

“In exchange,” Maerko said, “I don’t take eighteen.”

The room went still.

Senator T’oni did not move. Jag did not move. Kyp’s right hand, which had been flat, curled a quarter-inch on the table and was deliberately, slowly, flattened again.

Maerko held up both hands, palms out. Small. Mock.

“I’m not threatening you,” he said. “I’m telling you what the next quarter looks like if we don’t do a deal. I’ve got a lane already cut to the eighteenth. I’ve got the carriers for it. I’ve got the manpower for it. I’d rather not. I’d rather sit on the seventeen for five years and *build* the seventeen, because the seventeen are mine and they need roads and grain and clinics, and I’ve only got so many engineers, and every engineer I send into the eighteenth is an engineer I’m not putting under a roof on Hexani Forks Hill. So I’m proposing —” He let his hands down. “— I’m proposing that we agree. You agree. I sit on seventeen. I get my lanes. You don’t have to spend three fleets a year keeping me out of the eighteenth.”

“And the stormtrooper program,” T’oni said.

Maerko looked at her.

“What about it,” he said.

“The Galactic Alliance’s position —”

“Senator,” Maerko said. “Eat a dick.”

T’oni blinked. Once. Slowly.

“I was a trooper,” Maerko said. “Still am, Senator. The man on your colonnade, your shuttle, your gate? That’s me. That’s the same man I was four years ago in barracks on Hexani V with a bunk and a footlocker and TK-2208 on the front of my breastplate. I am not standing down the program. The program is the army. I’m not standing down the army. Move on.”

“Lord Maerko —”

“Move on, Senator. Truly. I’ll sit here and chew over the lanes with you until midnight. I will not chew over the army. The army stays.”

T’oni said, after a moment, “Noted.”

“Good.”

“What about the Jedi.”

The room changed.

It did not change loudly. Nobody moved. Nobody’s voice altered. But the quality of the air over the long table dropped one careful degree, the way the temperature drops one careful degree when a door has opened somewhere in the building and not yet been closed.

Maerko sat back. He folded his arms over his chest. He looked, again, at each of them — Jag, Jaina, Kyp — in turn. He looked longest at Kyp. Then he looked at Jaina, and held it.

“Which Jedi,” he said.

“The ones,” T’oni said, “you have taken.”

“Prisoners,” Maerko said.

“Yes.”

“POWs.”

“Call them what you like.”

“POWs,” Maerko said again. “They’re POWs, Senator. They are alive. I have got four of them. They are not, as far as the Alliance is concerned, slaves. The Confederation does not operate slavery, that is in the brief, and you can pick up the brief and read it if you like.” He looked at Jaina. “One of them you’d know.”

Jaina’s shoulders did not move.

“Kallos Station,” she said.

“Yeah.”

“Veya Solinn.”

Maerko was, briefly, surprised. It moved across the back of his eyes and then it was gone.

“You know her,” he said.

“I knew of her,” Jaina said. “I taught a seminar her cohort sat in on. Once. Five years ago. She corrected me on a translation of *Tythos*. She was right. I marked her for it.”

“Yeah,” Maerko said. “Sounds like her.”

He turned his head.

“Karet,” he said.

The young woman at the samovar had finished pouring out the tray. She was already, in fact, walking it across the floor — but she was not the only person now coming toward the table. From a door Jaina had not seen, in the back wall, behind the comm board, another figure had stepped out.

She was a woman of about Jaina’s height. She was wearing a tunic and trousers in the same plain working grey as the staff. She had dark hair cropped short. She walked in even, careful steps, the way a person walks who has been told to walk in even, careful steps, and around her throat, plain against the collar of the tunic, was a thin band of dull metal.

A collar.

It was not ornate. It was not the gaudy thing the holo-thrillers liked. It was a flat band, well-fitted, the seam at the side, the indicator a single dot at the back of the neck where she could not get at it. The dot was unlit.

Veya Solinn put the second tray down at the head of the table without spilling anything. She did not look up at Jaina, and she did not look up at Kyp, and she did not look up at Maerko. She set the tea things out the way a person sets tea things out who has done it a hundred times.

She straightened.

She stood, hands at the sides of her thighs, eyes on the middle distance, and waited to be dismissed.

Kyp Durron’s breath came out of him very slowly. Jaina could hear it.

“Karet,” Maerko said. He did not look at her. He was looking at Jaina, and watching her watch Veya. “Pull up a chair. Sit down. The Master’ll want to talk to you in a minute, I’d rather you were sitting.”

Veya did not move.

“Karet,” Maerko said.

“My name,” Veya said, “is Veya.”

“Yeah,” Maerko said. He looked, finally, sideways at her. “I know what your name is. Sit down. Please.”

Veya pulled out the chair next to him. She sat.

Maerko picked up his cup. He took a sip. He set it down.

“Right,” he said, conversationally. “We were doing the lanes.”
