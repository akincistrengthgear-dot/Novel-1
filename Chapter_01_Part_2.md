# Chapter 1 — The Audience at Hexani V

*Part 2 of 3*

---

He picked up his coffee mug, found it still empty, and put it down again on the same ring he had put it down on the last four times.

"Right," he said. "Phased release. I'm going to skip ahead to the part where I tell you the price, because we're all grown-ups and I don't want to walk you through the foreplay. Standard wartime rates for the rank-and-file. Pilots cost more. Anyone you held above colonel grade — and you held about a hundred — we negotiate one at a time, by name. I'll send Section One in with a list. I don't haggle, I don't bundle, I don't horse-trade for moral concessions. You pay or you don't."

"Cash?" T'oni said.

"Credits, gemstone, processed tibanna at spot. I'll take Hapan promissory at sixty cents on the credit. I won't take Alliance bonds because Alliance bonds are paper, and the paper says *trust me*, and we don't."

"Noted," T'oni said.

"Then the Jedi."

Jaina said nothing. She had been waiting for him to bring himself to it.

"I want her off my hands," he said. The frown deepened. Not hostility — something more like a man considering a piece of furniture he had owned for too long and was now obliged to defend in front of relatives. "She's not — look. I'm not going to pretend I'm doing anyone any favours. She kicked. She broke three of my men's necks at Kallos before they got the cuffs on her, and there's two more she ruined for life. I have her in a collar because she earned the collar. But she's not — she's not what your Order says she is. She's not a hostage. She's not bait. She's a person in my house who pours my tea, and I don't sell people, so."

Behind him, very softly, Vesi Tellan said, "Pours your tea."

He closed his eyes.

"I pour the tea."

"You pour the tea," he agreed.

"He cannot," she said to the table at large, in the same flat hospitality voice she had used to hand around the cups, "find his own kettle. He once tried to brew sapir in a soup pot."

"That happened *one time*."

"It happened twice."

"It happened *one time* in the office. The galley does not count."

"The galley counts."

He turned in his chair. He did not turn far — he was not a man who pivoted a whole body when his neck would do the work — and he gave her a look across his shoulder that was meant to be quelling and was not.

"Knight Tellan," he said, "if you have a contribution to make to the negotiation, you may sit down at the table."

"I have a contribution to make to the truth of the matter."

"The truth of the matter is not on the agenda."

"It's not?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it. The face went through a small private weather system between *retort* and *retort denied by sergeant-instincts of not actually being in the right*, and then the words that came out of him were —

"You — listen — that's not — that — *that's* —"

"That's what?" Vesi said.

"That's —"

"That's —" Vesi echoed, the cadence and the broken pitch of it exact, "that's *that's* —"

A small flat sound came out of the side console. The bald junior tech at the comms board, who had been sitting motionless for the last ten minutes with the rigid agony of a man being paid not to listen, had snorted through his nose.

Maerko's head whipped around.

"Stop laughing," he said. "You're not even a person. You're a *testicle.*"

The tech put a hand over his mouth and turned his entire body toward the wall.

Jag Fel, beside Jaina, made a small choking noise. He had been in the middle of a swallow of tea. The swallow had not, technically speaking, completed itself. He set the cup down on the table with the careful precision of a man who would, given five more seconds, be all right.

Jaina permitted the corner of her mouth to move. Not a smile. A concession. A diplomatic acknowledgement that something funny had happened, made by a woman who was not going to give Jiyad Maerko the gift of a full one.

Maerko caught it anyway. He looked at her, and his frown re-formed itself around something that was almost, but not quite, *vindicated*.

"See," he said to the table. "She thought it was funny."

"I did not," Jaina said.

"You did."

"Lord Maerko —"

"Knight, you absolutely —"

The far door opened.

Eron Vass came back in. He was walking faster than he had walked out. His datapad was no longer clutched against his chest; it was held in front of him at the angle of a man delivering a writ. He crossed the office in four strides, came up at Maerko's right elbow, and bent and put his mouth very close to Maerko's ear.

Vass spoke for a long time. Maerko's expression did not change for the first eight seconds and then darkened across the next six. By the time Vass straightened, Maerko had his fingers pressed against the bridge of his nose and the rest of his face arranged in the particular shape of a man who was about to swear at length in a language he had been trying to stop swearing in.

He did not, in front of company, swear. He spoke quietly to Vass.

He should have remembered the Jedi at the table.

Jaina did not have to reach for it. The Force on Hexani V was wrong in the way Corran had warned her it would be wrong, but she had grown up hearing things she was not meant to hear, and her enhanced hearing did not require the Force at all; it required only that she had been Jacen Solo's sister, and Jacen had taught her to listen.

She caught half of it.

*— jumped-up Trandoshan lizard — put a team together —*

*— hunt him down to whatever rock —*

*— and you cut off his nutsack, Vass, you make sure of it, and you feed it to him, and you have somebody recording the whole thing, I want it on a chip on my desk by the weekend, I want to hear him scream —*

*— the freighter schedule, the depot, and the Jedi, in that order —*

Beside her, Corran had gone very still. Corran had heard it too.

Vesi rolled her eyes.

It was a small, expert eye-roll, the kind a person developed over two years of standing three paces behind somebody and listening to the work-day of the galaxy's most prolific cusser. The roll passed over her face like weather and was gone.

Maerko caught it.

"Don't *listen,*" he said to her, without turning.

"I'm not."

"You absolutely are."

"I am holding a tray, my Lord."

"You are holding a tray and *listening*. Stop."

"If I stop, you will spend the next hour explaining things to me I already know."

"Fine, then keep listening, and stop having opinions about it."

"I will have an opinion."

"Vesi."

"I will *piss in your teacup*, my Lord, the next time you call me *Knight Tellan* in front of the Alliance like I'm a soldier at parade."

"I will demote you."

"You cannot demote me. I have no rank in your army."

"I will demote you to *piss-pot polisher*. It's a new rank. I'm inventing it."

"Then I will piss in *more* of your cups."

"*Shoo,*" Maerko said. "Out. Out of the room. Take the tray. Out."

Vesi turned, with the tray, and went out through the door she had come in by. She did not, in passing, look at Jaina.

The far door sealed behind her.

There were several seconds of very thorough quiet.

Jiyad Maerko set both palms flat on the table, looked at his palms as if they had personally offended him, and then looked up.

"Knight Solo Fel," he said. "Head of State Fel. Senator. Master. I owe you an apology."

He stood.

"Something has come up that I have to put my hands on personally. I'd like to break for an hour. Vass will have you taken to a lounge, you'll be fed if you want feeding, the kaf is fresh. I'm not stalling, and I'm not theatre. When I come back I'll come back ready to talk territory, the moratorium, and the freighter clauses, and we'll be done by sundown. Is an hour acceptable."

"It's acceptable," Jaina said.

"Good." He nodded once. "Vass."

"My Lord."

"Send for the duty motor. I want to be at the South Depot in ten minutes." He paused, and then, as an afterthought, with the particular flat detachment that Jaina was already cataloguing as his most dangerous tone — "And get Vesi from the corridor. She's coming with me."

He walked out.

Vass bowed his head a quarter-inch toward the delegation, made an apologetic gesture toward the door he had originally entered by, and the four of them stood and followed him out into the corridor.

---

The lounge was on the second floor, two corridors over from the office, and it was the first room in the complex that had any furniture in it that had not been chosen by an engineer.

There were four armchairs in the lounge. They did not match. One of them was Imperial-issue, the sort of broad squared-off thing meant to make a sector commander look larger than he was; one was made of dark Mygeeto teak with a kind of beaten brass riveting; one was upholstered in flat Hapan velvet and had clearly been looted, given the dust along its piping; and one was a battered repulsor-couch that did not repulse anymore, the gravity unit underneath it dead and cold and serving now only as a footrest. They were arranged around a low table on which sat a fresh pot of kaf, four clean cups, a plate of bread, a plate of cold mottled-grey meat that Jaina did not investigate, and a bowl of dark sticky fruit she recognised as muja paste.

A single window ran the length of the far wall. It looked out, not on the field they had landed at, but on the inner courtyard of the complex. Jaina counted six guards posted around the perimeter and three drones loitering in the air above. The drones moved in a slow lazy ellipse that did not, on second look, repeat exactly.

Vass left them at the door. "Buzz the wall panel if you need anything. There is a refresher through the small door. The lounge is not bugged."

"Of course it's bugged," T'oni said.

"It is not bugged today, Senator. I had it stripped yesterday afternoon. I will not lie to you about which days. Today it is not bugged."

He bowed again, more formally this time, and went out. The door sealed.

Nobody sat down for a moment.

Jag walked the perimeter. He did not pretend to be doing anything other than walking the perimeter — he was the husband of a Solo and he had been a fighter pilot his whole life, and he did not have it in him to pretend a room was harmless when he had not yet checked it. He looked at the ceiling vents. He looked at the wall panel. He looked behind the curtain.

Corran sat down in the Mygeeto teak chair, very slowly, as a man with bad knees does. He did not pour kaf. He closed his eyes.

T'oni walked to the window, folded her arms, and watched the drones.

Jaina poured kaf into four cups. She put a cup at Corran's elbow. She put a cup on the broad arm of the Imperial chair for Jag when he was done with his sweep. She took a cup for herself, and one for T'oni, and joined T'oni at the window.

"He is what we were told he is," T'oni said quietly.

"And about a quarter of what we weren't told."

"Yes."

The Bothan tipped her cup toward the field. "He has rebuilt this in six years."

"Five and a half."

"You're counting from the coup."

"I'm counting from the coup."

"Han would say that was a lot of building to do in five and a half years."

"Han would say a lot of things." Jaina sipped her kaf. It was good kaf. It was somebody else's kaf, somebody who knew kaf. "He'd also say count the warehouses."

T'oni's small dusty-cream ear turned a quarter toward her. "Count the warehouses."

"He always said it about smugglers' ports. Count the warehouses, not the ships. The ships move. The warehouses are the truth."

T'oni considered the line of warehouse roofs visible above the inner walls of the complex. There were, Jaina had counted, eleven that she could see; there were more she could not. They were not all military. Half of them were stencilled with the same six-hexagon mark she had seen on the medical pallets, and half were stencilled with civilian commercial marks — grain, tibanna, drysteel.

"He is not building an army," T'oni said. "He is building a country."

"He's building an army inside a country."

"Yes."

Behind them, Jag finished his perimeter, picked up his kaf, and sat in the Imperial chair. He did not slump in it. He sat the way an Imperial colonel sat in an Imperial chair, which was to say, the chair had been designed for him and it knew it.

"He's stronger than the brief said," Jag said.

"Yes," Jaina said.

"He's also more tired."

"Yes."

"That's worth more than the strength."

Jaina turned from the window. "Say it."

"Tired men make peace," Jag said. "Strong men don't. A man who's both is — useful, if you can get a clause in front of him in the hour he's willing to sign."

"And if you don't?"

"Then you've got the second hour, in which he's a strong man who didn't sleep enough."

Jaina turned this over. The shape of it was familiar; the man it had come out of was not. Jag was Imperial through and through, and Imperial men her age had not learned, until very recently, how to speak about tired warlords with the cold neutral kindness of a doctor diagnosing the disease.

"You're worried about him," she said.

"I'm worried *for* him." Jag set the cup down. "There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"There is, today. Tomorrow, who knows."

T'oni, still at the window, made the considering sound she had been making all morning. She had not turned, but she had heard.

"He gives her a collar," T'oni said. "And she answers him back. And he lets her."

"Yes," Jaina said.

"That is not a master. That is a man who is afraid to be alone in a room."

"Yes."

"That is also not a tactical asset I know how to negotiate with."

"No."

Corran opened his eyes. "He will sign the trade lanes."

"You think?"

"He will sign the trade lanes because he needs the freighter access more than he needs us to like him. He won't budge on the territory. He'll talk about the Jedi for a while because it's the part he's actually angry about. He'll trade the prisoners in pieces, slowly, and he'll use each piece to extract something." Corran tipped his cup at the ceiling. "He's a sergeant, Jaina. He's not a politician. Sergeants don't lie to their men, they lie *for* their men. He'll do the same thing for his country. Everything he tells us today will be true, in the sense that he'll mean it when he says it, and he'll renegotiate the meaning the moment we leave the room."

T'oni's fur lifted on the back of her neck.

"That is a very generous way of describing a liar," she said.

"He's not a liar. He's a sergeant. There's a difference. The difference is that a sergeant tells you the truth as it stands in the hour he's saying it, and he doesn't take responsibility for whether the hour holds."

"The same way Han doesn't," Jag said, without inflection.

Jaina did not turn to look at him.

"The same way Han doesn't," Corran agreed. "Yes."

She drank her kaf.

She watched a Hexani drone trace its long lazy ellipse over the inner courtyard, and she thought, not for the first time in the last hour, about Vesi Tellan in the corridor outside Maerko's office, walking away from the room with the empty tray held flat against her hip, the way somebody held a tray they had been holding their whole life.

She had not looked at Jaina. Not once.

That was, Jaina was fairly sure, a message.

She had not yet decided what kind.

---

Vesi Tellan had spent the first six months of her captivity refusing to learn the layout of the building.

She had told herself, those first six months, that she was about to escape. That she would not give the Force the satisfaction of letting her notice the geometry of her own cage. That a Jedi did not survey her prison; a Jedi waited for the opportunity to leave it, and learned only what she needed to learn to walk out the door when the door opened.

The door had not opened.

Now, eighteen months further on, she knew every door. She knew the door from the office to the corridor and from the corridor to the lift, and the door of the lift, and the doors at the bottom of the lift where the motor pool waited; she knew the door of the speeder, which closed with a particular soft *thock* because the speeder was very expensive and very old and had been looted from a Hapan dignitary in a campaign she did not let herself remember; she knew the doors of the South Depot, three layers of them, the outermost a bare durasteel slab on a hinge, the middle a thicker blast-door on rails, and the innermost a vault door with five separate locks set into its face, each lock keyed to a different officer.

She knew the depot.

She was standing in it now, two paces behind Maerko's right shoulder, which was where she had learned, over time, that he wanted her — close enough to hand him a datapad if he asked for one, far enough that she could not stab him in the kidney without taking a step first. He had never said as much. He had simply, by his body, taught her the line. Two paces. She had not, in fifteen months of being walked at heel through every secure facility in the Confederation, ever tested it.

The air in the depot was cold. The air everywhere on Hexani V was cold; the air here was colder, by design, because cold air did not encourage volatile compounds to volatilise. There was a chemical smell underneath the cold that the climate system did not quite catch — a sharp dry note, like ozone with a knife in it.

And there was the array.

The null array in the South Depot was not the small portable thing the trooper had carried on the landing pad. It was a fixed installation: a column of grey-ceramic plates running floor to ceiling at each corner of the inner vault, and the field they made between them was the thickest she had ever stood inside. The Force, in here, did not lie to her so much as *hum*. It hummed the way a comlink hummed when the comlink was open to nobody — a flat dead carrier signal that swallowed every attempt to reach into it. She had stopped reaching. She had stopped reaching the first time she had been brought here, eight months ago, when she had quietly tried to call her lightsaber across the room from the small cabinet where Maerko kept it, and the Force had returned to her not the saber but a faint sour pulse, like the taste of metal in the back of the mouth.

She had not tried again.

She used her eyes instead.

The vault was the size of a small cargo bay. Pallets ran in lanes along the walls and down the centre of the room, each pallet draped in matte black sheeting, each sheeting stencilled with an alphanumeric tag and a date of last inspection. The dates ran back. Two months ago. Six months ago. A year. Five years. *Twenty-three* years. *Eighty-six* years, in faded Imperial dating, on a row of crates at the far end whose tags had been overpainted three times.

And the very far row. The row in the deepest corner. The row Maerko was walking her toward.

The tags on that row were not in Imperial. They were in *Mando'a.*

She could not read Mando'a. She had been to enough briefings in her life to recognise the script — the angular runes, the small dot-cluster prefixes, the right-to-left orientation. The crates themselves were stamped with a symbol she did know: a stylised mythosaur skull, deep-bitten into the durasteel, the lines softened by what had to be several thousand years of careful storage.

Maerko stopped in front of the Mando crates. He put one hand, flat, on the lid of the nearest one. He left it there a moment, as if greeting an old dog.

"Hello, beautiful," he said quietly. "Still in one piece?"

The Trandoshan refit-foreman beside him snorted. "Lord. Yes, Lord."

"How's the core?"

"Stable. We pulled the lead jacket last week. The fissile's all there. About seventy percent original yield, we figure. Tibanna fund's bought us new propellant for the delivery shells — when those go in, you'll get a clean nine-tenths of the rated detonation. Maybe more, if we re-pack the secondary."

"And the secondary's the gas, not the boom?"

"Secondary's the gas, Lord. Mando design. They wrapped the kinetic and the chemical in the same casing. Detonates the kinetic first, dumps the gas through the heat-flash. Single bird, two horrors."

"Beautiful," Maerko said again, soft and almost fond. "*Beautiful.* Show me the dispersal."

The foreman did. He led Maerko down the row, and Maerko nodded along, and Vesi followed at two paces.

She was, very carefully, not listening.

She was listening.

She did not have the Force in here. She had her eyes, her ears, her training, and the years of practice she had now in keeping her face flat in the presence of horrors that other Jedi got to recoil from. The crates went by. The numbers went by. Forty-eight Mandalorian baradium charges of various yields, the smallest a hand-portable demolition and the largest a city-killer. Twelve thermonuclear devices of late Imperial manufacture, salvaged from a discontinued garrison stockpile she did not let herself name. Six vats of what the foreman called *Trihex-eleven-thirty-eight* in a voice that suggested he himself did not know what was in them. A pallet of nerve-agent shells in Imperial inventory codes she had not seen since reading a Yuuzhan Vong war briefing during her apprentice exams. And, in the very deepest corner, behind its own steel grille and its own separate null-array column —

A bioweapon.

She knew what it was because the crate was marked, in Basic, with a stencilled skull and the words *DO NOT OPEN. EVER.*

She looked away.

Maerko had finished his walk. He came back along the row with the foreman, and at the bioweapon crate he paused, and he did not put his hand on it. He looked at it for perhaps half a second longer than was comfortable, and he kept walking.

He stopped at the door of the vault. He turned, and he saw her looking at him.

For the first time since they had landed in the motor pool, Jiyad Maerko looked, fully and directly, at Vesi Tellan.

"You all right?" he said.

She held his eyes.

"Define *all right*," she said.

"Standing-up all right. I know the rest is a stretch."

"Standing up."

"Good." He jerked his head at the door. "Eat something on the way back. There's nut bars in the speeder pocket. You skipped breakfast."

"I poured your breakfast."

"You skipped your own. Eat a nut bar."

He turned and walked out.

She followed him at two paces, because that was where he wanted her, and because in the door of the vault, with five locks closing behind her on what she had just seen, she had not yet decided what else to do.

---

He was eight minutes late coming back.

He came into the office through the same far door, alone, with a different coffee mug in his hand and his sleeves rolled now to the elbow rather than the mid-forearm. The change in the sleeves was the only thing that suggested he had been somewhere. His frown was the same. His hair was the same. The faint chemical smell that came off his shoulders, when he passed Jaina's chair, was not.

"Sorry," he said. "Truly. I'm an hour and eight minutes late on an hour break, and I owe you the eight." He sat. He set the mug on the same ring. "Where were we."

"You hadn't told us where you'd been," Jag said.

"No. I hadn't." Maerko looked at him across the table, mildly. "Are you asking?"

"I'm asking."

"South Depot. I had to walk it personally. Once a quarter I walk every depot we have, top to bottom, and I look at every crate, and I read every tag, and I sign the inspection myself, because the day I outsource that walk is the day I get one of my own staff selling me to Coruscant for a knighthood. Today was the South Depot's day."

"What's in the South Depot?" T'oni asked.

The question was put very mildly.

Maerko picked up his coffee mug. He turned it once on its ring, idly, the way a man rotated a chess piece he had not yet committed to moving.

"Nerve gas," he said. "Nukes. Some other things."

There was a quiet, sustained moment.

He waited it out without looking pleased.

"You have *nuclear weapons,*" T'oni said.

"Twelve."

"You have *twelve nuclear weapons.*"

"Twelve thermonuclear devices, yes, Senator. Late-Imperial garrison stock, originally manufactured in the second decade after Endor, decommissioned by the Pellaeon government around the time it was making nice with Coruscant, sold off as scrap to a contractor my Section Three later bought, transported home in pieces in tibanna shells. I have the bills of sale. They are very boring bills of sale."

"You have nerve gas."

"I have nerve gas. I have several different nerve gases, in fact. I have the old Imperial Trihex stocks, I have some unpleasant Republic-era things we found in a hold on Lannik, and I have a number of items considerably older than that."

"How much older."

"Some of them were sealed by Mandalorians, Senator, when the Mandalorians were last *really* kicking in force against the Sith. I would estimate the older end of my collection at about four thousand years. It's pretty. Some of the crates still have the mythosaur on them."

Jag had set down his cup again. Jag was holding very, very still.

T'oni's fur was up along her shoulders and the back of her arms. "Lord Maerko —"

"Senator."

"Are you *telling us this on purpose.*"

He drank his coffee.

"Yes."

He set the mug down.

"I'm telling you because the tibanna credits have been very good to us this year, Senator. We've had the budget to do refurbishment we did not have last year. Lead jackets are coming off. Old propellant is coming out. New propellant is going in. The Mando birds are coming up to about nine-tenths of their rated yield. The Imperial twelve will be ready inside a year. I am telling you all of this on purpose because I would like it on the Senate record, in your hand, that *Jiyad Maerko has been honest with us about this.* You'll forget I was honest, eventually. Your successor won't read the file. But I want it on the file."

"You are telling us," T'oni said, "that you have nuclear weapons, that you have nerve gas, that you are refurbishing them, and that you are doing this *on purpose.*"

"I am telling you that all the more reason for the Alliance to leave me alone."

He let that sit on the table.

Then he leaned forward, very slightly, and the frown did the small involuntary thing his face did when he was, despite himself, *enjoying* the moment.

"You see, Senator," he said, mild, almost cheerful, "I have wondered. *Quite* a bit. Whether people really do turn to ash when a thermo goes off close enough. There's old footage from the Atrisian wars, but the footage is grainy. The literature is mixed. I've read everything I could get my hands on and I still don't know. I'd very much like to find out. I am hoping — *praying*, Senator — that I never get the chance to find out. But if your Alliance pushes the line in twenty months when the moratorium ends, and your fleet comes over my border, then I will have my chance, and I'll satisfy my curiosity, and a number of your soldiers will satisfy it for me. That's the offer. Take it. Leave me alone."

The room was very quiet.

Jaina did not move.

She had been raised by a princess, and by a smuggler, and the smuggler had taught her how to recognise a bluff and the princess had taught her how to recognise a man who was not bluffing, and the man across the table from her — sleeves at the elbow, coffee mug on its ring, the small chemical smell of the South Depot still on his shoulders — was not bluffing.

He was waiting to see which one of them was going to speak first.

She let him wait.
