# Chapter One — *The Lord of Hexani*
## Part 2 of 3

---

The second cup of tea did not get poured.

Maerko had just lifted the pot off the tray — he was, Jaina noted, the only person in the room who poured for himself, which she would later realise was deliberate — when the small door behind the comm board opened again and a man in a working tunic stepped through and stopped at the threshold like a man who had learned, somewhere, that you did not walk into a room. You waited at its edge.

Maerko did not look up. He poured. He set the pot down. He glanced at the man over the rim of the cup.

“Soris,” he said.

“My Lord.”

“What.”

“A word, my Lord.”

“Got the senator at the table.”

“Yes, my Lord.”

“And?”

“A word, my Lord.”

Maerko held the cup near his mouth. He looked at his aide for the count of two. The aide, Soris, did not move. The aide had a small flimsi in his right hand, folded in half along its short side, and the small flimsi had a band of red printed along the top edge. Jaina knew that band. The Imperial Remnant used the same one. *Priority, sealed source, eyes-only.*

Maerko set the cup down.

“Right,” he said. “‘Scuse me.”

He stood up. He stood up the way he had sat down — without ceremony, the chair complaining, his coat hitched briefly on the chair back so that he had to give it a small tug to free it. He gave the table a half-nod that was as close as he came to a bow.

“Sorry,” he said. “Won’t be a minute. Karet. Topping up. Senator, the *kaffi* on the second tray is the local; try it if you’ve got the stomach, it’s rough but it’s ours. Your Majesty, I’ll send for proper black if you’d rather. Master Solo Fel — ” He glanced once at Jaina. The frown had not moved. The eyes had. “ — your kid sister is welcome. Just so you know. If she ever wants the tour. No ambush.”

Jaina’s mouth opened. It did not produce anything.

Maerko was already past the table.

“Soris.” He angled his chin at the back door. “Through. Now.”

The aide stepped aside. Maerko went past him. The door shut.

For a moment, no one in the room moved.

Then the young woman at the samovar — *Karet*, the name Maerko kept reaching for and missing — set down the cup she had been about to fill, gave the table a small, polite, almost shy nod, and went back to her station to wait. Veya Solinn, in the chair at Maerko’s right hand, sat with her hands folded in her lap and her gaze on a point about halfway down the long table, neither far nor near. She had not spoken since she had said *my name is Veya*. Her shoulders were even. Her breathing was even. She was, Jaina realised after a beat, doing breath-work — the slow even four-count Master Skywalker had taught the seminar room five years ago when the cohort had been Veya’s age. *Hold. Release. Hold. Release.* The Force around her was a small clean flame inside the wider stuttering mess of the room’s ambient null. She was carrying her quiet. The collar had not taken it from her.

Jaina’s chest did a thing she did not let on her face.

“Senator,” she said. Quiet. “Your call.”

T’oni was already moving. T’oni had the folder back open on the table, two fingers on the top page, the same easy practical motion she had used to close it. She did not look up.

“We wait,” T’oni said.

“We wait,” Kyp said. He did not say it like a question.

“We wait,” T’oni said, “and we don’t talk shop in this room.”

“This room,” Jag said, “is wired.”

“Yes, Your Majesty.”

“Wired well.”

“Yes.”

“Then we wait,” Jag said.

Kyp leaned his elbows on the table and put his fingers together at the bridge of his nose. He breathed once, slow, through his nose. He looked, briefly, sideways, at Veya. Veya did not look back.

A second man came through the back door — older, thin, the stylus still tucked behind his ear from the topographical plinth — and stopped at the table. He did not look at the delegation. He looked at Veya.

“Karet,” he said. “Walk with me.”

Veya stood up. She did not look at Jaina. She did not look at anyone. She went around the chair, and around the table, and out through the small door behind the comm board, and the door shut behind her, and she was gone.

The room held very still.

“Senator,” Soris said. He had stayed at the threshold. He had a small, formal voice, like the voice of a man who had been trained to be a senior aide and was wearing the training even now. “His Lordship begs an hour. There is a — there is a matter at one of the depots. He would not delay you for less. He sends his apologies.”

“An hour,” T’oni said.

“Less, if possible, ma’am.”

“What kind of matter.”

“A criminal one, ma’am.”

T’oni waited.

Soris did not elaborate.

“Sergeant,” Soris said, into his earpiece. He had not raised his voice. “Antechamber prepared. Senator’s party. Refreshments through. The good water.” He inclined his head to the table. “Senator. If you’ll come with me. There’s a sitting room across the hall. Fire’s laid. It’s a more comfortable wait than this one. Karet will bring the tea through.”

T’oni stood. She closed the folder again.

“Thank you,” she said.

“Ma’am.”

They followed Soris out.

The sitting room was, Jaina suspected, the old governor’s study from before the coup.

It had the bones of an Imperial governor’s study, anyway — the high panelled walls, the carved sill above a long arched window, the iron sconces that had once held flame and had been refitted, badly, for plasma. The carpet was new. The chairs were not Imperial; they were Hexani, the same plain working make as the chairs at the long table, and somebody had pushed three of them and a low settle into a half-circle around the hearth. The hearth had a real fire in it. The fire had been laid against the chill that had begun to come down the canyon as the afternoon shadow lengthened. The flue drew well.

The window looked north and up. The cliff above the city showed at this height, raw rock and old machine cut, a single banner running on a pole at the rim. The slag-and-burn slash, again. Two more troopers on the rim. Honour guards, or watch. Both.

T’oni took the settle. Jag took the chair at her right. Jaina took the chair at her left. Kyp did not sit.

Kyp walked to the window. He put one hand flat on the sill. He looked up at the cliff.

“His sister,” he said.

Jaina did not turn her head.

“My sister,” she said. “Yes.”

“He knows about Allana.”

“He knows my mother carried twins. He knows my brother had a daughter. He knows my sister-in-law is who she is. He has a good intelligence service, Master Durron, we already knew that from the brief. He flagged it to me because he wanted me to know he knows.”

“He flagged it to you,” Kyp said, “because he wanted to see if you’d twitch.”

“Yes.”

“Did you twitch.”

“No,” she said.

“You did, slightly.”

“I did, slightly. I’m human, Master Durron.”

Kyp did not turn around.

“I don’t like him,” he said.

“I know.”

“I don’t like him *more* than I expected to not like him.”

“I know.”

“He is — ” Kyp let go of the sill. He turned. The fire put a strip of light up one side of his face. “He is not what they wrote in the brief. The brief has a tyrant. There is a tyrant in this building. The brief — the brief has him in robes, Jaina. With a guard. The brief has a man sitting on a chair on a dais waiting for us to be impressed. Did you read that brief.”

“I read that brief.”

“He is sitting in shirtsleeves,” Kyp said, “at his own table, and pouring his own tea, and joking about lemon, because he has worked out, somewhere along the way, that if he sits in shirtsleeves at his own table and pours his own tea and jokes about lemon, then anybody he ever has lashed to a wall and flogged to death is somebody he has lashed to a wall and flogged to death in a uniform we will look at later, and not somebody he has lashed to a wall and flogged to death between this cup of tea and the next cup of tea. He has it down. He has practised. This is a *trained* man, Jaina. Don’t — ” He stopped himself. He breathed out. “Don’t mistake the shirtsleeves.”

“I didn’t mistake the shirtsleeves, Kyp.”

“You laughed.”

“I didn’t laugh.”

“You almost laughed.”

“I almost laughed.”

“Don’t even almost laugh.”

“Master Durron,” Jaina said. Carefully. Quietly. “I have buried three Knights of this Order to the war on his frontier. I am going to bury more before this room shuts. Don’t do me the favour of telling me what I am almost doing.”

Kyp opened his mouth.

“Master Durron,” T’oni said.

Kyp shut his mouth.

T’oni had not raised her voice. T’oni was still sitting with the folder on her knee. She had not opened it. She was looking into the fire.

“He’s good,” T’oni said.

She said it to the fire.

“He is very good,” T’oni said. “The colonnade. The chair. The kid at the samovar. The brief was not — the brief was not wrong about the man, Master Durron. The brief was lazy about the *show*. He has a show. The show is *no show*. He sits in his shirtsleeves. He pours his own tea. He calls us by our names. He calls his prisoners by their names. He has a Jedi in a collar pour my tea and apologise for the lemon, and somewhere in the next ten minutes he expects me to tell my staff that he is a reasonable man. He is not a reasonable man, Master Durron, and I know he is not a reasonable man, and yet —”

She stopped.

“Yet,” Jag said.

“Yet,” T’oni said.

“Yes,” Jag said.

Jaina laid her hand, briefly, on T’oni’s. She did not press. She lifted again.

“He told my husband,” Jaina said, “that he was a trooper. He used the present tense. *Still is.* He told me his name. He used the present tense again. He told you he picked *Lord* because it was the short word and shooting himself was the price of the long one. He has not lied to us yet. Senator, listen to me. He has not lied to us. That is what is wrong.”

“Yes,” T’oni said.

“He has told us, very straight, what he wants, and he has told us, very straight, that he will burn the eighteenth system if we don’t give it to him, and he poured his own tea while he said it, and *that* — ” She let her hand rest on the arm of her chair. “That is the show, Kyp. The show is that he doesn’t need a show. He has the army for it.”

Kyp said, low, “Then he has the army for it.”

“Yes,” Jaina said.

“And we go home with the deal.”

“Maybe.”

“Or we go home with the war.”

“Yes.”

“Senator,” Kyp said. “Tell me the Alliance is going to swallow the seventeen.”

T’oni did not answer immediately.

“The Alliance,” she said, slowly, “has a hawk caucus that won’t. The Alliance has a pragmatist caucus that will. The Alliance has Cal Omas in the middle, who is tired. I am here as the pragmatist caucus, Master Durron, with Chief of State Omas’s permission, to find out whether the seventeen are *worth* swallowing.”

“And.”

“And so far,” T’oni said, “I believe they are.”

She said it without any pleasure.

Kyp turned back to the window.

Jag, who had not yet spoken, spoke now. He spoke in the small flat tone he kept for matters he had been turning over for a while and did not, particularly, want to turn over out loud.

“The kit,” Jag said.

T’oni looked at him.

“Your Majesty.”

“The kit on every man,” Jag said. “The shield on every back. The null array, one per platoon, a man can carry it on his shoulder. This is not a remnant warlord, Senator. A remnant warlord cannot make the kit. A remnant warlord *steals* the kit. He has factories, somewhere, for the shields. He has factories for the array. He is not stealing them from the Empire because the Empire would know, and I am the Empire, Senator, and the Empire does not.” He laid his right hand on his knee. “He is making this kit himself, in his seventeen, and he is putting it on every common man in his line, and he has been doing this — ” He looked, briefly, at Jaina. “ — for longer than the brief says.”

Jaina nodded.

“Yes,” she said.

“The brief is lazy,” Jag said.

“Yes.”

“The brief is lazy at the *Alliance* level, Senator,” Jag said. “Which means the Alliance is lazy. Which means Cal Omas is being lazy.”

“Your Majesty,” T’oni said.

“This is not a criticism of the Chief of State, Senator.”

“It sounded like one.”

“It was one,” Jag said. “But it is not personal.”

T’oni almost smiled. The almost did not get on her face. The almost moved under it.

“Your Majesty,” she said. “Are you about to be helpful.”

“I am about to be honest,” Jag said. “I would prefer the Galactic Alliance not give him the seventeen.”

“Yes.”

“I will give the Galactic Alliance the support of the Empire if the Galactic Alliance chooses not to give him the seventeen.”

“Yes.”

“I will also,” Jag said, “tell you, here, in this room, that I do not believe the combined fleets of the Galactic Alliance and the Empire can take the seventeen back without a campaign of two years and a casualty bill no chamber of either of our governments will pay. And I will tell you that I believe that *he* — ” He nodded once at the wall behind which Maerko had gone. “ — believes the same thing, and does not say it, because saying it would be vulgar.”

T’oni said, after a beat, “Vulgar.”

“Yes,” Jag said.

“That is the word, Your Majesty.”

“It is, Senator.”

T’oni looked back at the fire.

The fire did not answer her. The fire was a working fire in a working hearth in a working room in a working keep in a working city in seventeen working systems run by a man in shirtsleeves who was, somewhere in this building, signing a writ to have a slaver dismembered for stealing from him, and who would come back through that door in the next forty minutes and pour her another cup of tea.

Jaina put her hand over her eyes. Not for long. Just for the breath of three.

When she took it down, T’oni was looking at her.

“Master Solo,” T’oni said. “The collar.”

“Yes,” Jaina said.

“Will you ask.”

“I will ask.”

“In front of him.”

“Yes.”

“He will refuse.”

“Probably.”

“Try anyway.”

“I will.”

“Try a system,” T’oni said.

Jaina’s head came up.

“We will not,” T’oni said, slowly, “go home with the collared Jedi. Master Solo. We will not. I will not have it on the report. Cal Omas will not have it on the report. The Jedi Order will not have it on the report. Try a system. Try two. Try whatever you have to try. He is going to ask for the eighteenth system in exchange. Tell him yes.”

“Senator —”

“Tell him yes,” T’oni said. “I am authorising it. Across this table. With the Empire as my witness. Get her out, Master Solo.”

Kyp said, from the window, “She might not come.”

Three faces turned to him.

“She might not come,” Kyp said. “Did you watch her, in there. She has — she has the breath-work. She has the calm. She is *carrying* something. She is not a woman waiting to be rescued, Senator. She is a woman who has decided where to stand. We should be careful what we offer her.”

Jaina did not answer.

She knew Kyp was right. She did not, particularly, want him to be right.

The fire popped.

The door opened. Karet came in with the tea tray, head down, the way servants on stiff floors learned to come in. She set the tray on the low table between the chairs. She straightened. She glanced, very quickly, at Jaina, and looked down again, and went out.

She had been crying.

Just at the corner of her eye. Just a smear. Jaina caught it on the way down and did not, by an act of will, look at it twice.

“Senator,” Jaina said. “He has staff.”

“Yes,” T’oni said. She had not seen the smear. She was looking at the fire. “He has a great deal of staff, Master Solo.”

“No,” Jaina said. “I mean — he has *staff*. They’re not soldiers. They’re not slaves. They’re — they’re people. They’re working here.”

“Yes,” T’oni said.

“The kid at the samovar.”

“Yes.”

“She’s crying.”

T’oni looked up. T’oni had not seen.

“Because of us?” T’oni said.

“I don’t know,” Jaina said. “No. I don’t — I don’t think so. I don’t think she was crying about us.”

Jag said, without looking at any of them, “Karet was on the depot.”

Three faces turned to him.

“She came in,” Jag said, “behind the aide. She walked past the long table. I watched her hands. She was holding the cup wrong. She was holding it the way somebody holds something when somebody has just told them somebody they know is dead. Not a relative. A colleague. Not close. But — known.”

The room was quiet.

“The depot,” Jaina said.

“Yes,” Jag said.

“The one he just went to.”

“Yes.”

“The depot,” Jaina said, slowly, “that had a criminal matter at it.”

“Yes.”

“Has been hit.”

“Yes,” Jag said. “Karet is staff. Karet knew somebody on it. Soris would not have shown the senator the flimsi if it had only been a check. Maerko would not have stood up if it had only been a check. He stood up the way a man stands up when he has not been told a small thing.”

Kyp turned away from the window.

“So,” Kyp said. “Somebody got into his weapons depot.”

“Yes,” Jag said.

“And he is, right now, deciding how to put pieces of them on a wall.”

“Yes.”

“And he is coming back here,” Kyp said, “after.”

“Yes,” Jag said.

“Lovely,” Kyp said.

He sat down at last. He took the third chair. He set his hands on his knees. He looked, for the first time since they had landed, like a tired man.

T’oni poured the tea. She poured for all four of them. She did not ask. She handed Jaina the cup with the sugar already in it.

“Drink,” T’oni said.

Jaina drank.

The tea was rough and dark and good and very Hexani, made from a leaf the Outer Rim grew on slopes like the ones above this city, dried over tibanna flame, sharp at the back of the throat. Jaina drank half the cup. She set it down.

She let her eyes close. Just for the count of four. *Hold. Release. Hold. Release.* The same four-count Veya had been doing at the table. The same four-count Master Skywalker had taught a seminar room five years ago.

When she opened her eyes, Jag was watching her.

He did not say anything.

He laid his hand, briefly, on the back of hers. Just the palm. He did not press. He lifted again.

She let him.

Veya Solinn had a small flatcake of her own.

The galley behind the audience floor was not the one she usually worked out of — that one was on the staff level, three down, where the line cooks were — but it was the closest galley to the long table, and it was the one Karet had keyed her into when the bell had gone. The flatcake had been on the corner of the prep slab. Someone had left it there. Veya had eaten half of it standing up, in three bites, with her back to the door, because she had been standing for an hour and she had not eaten since fifth bell.

The Force was still on. The room hum was a constant low-key flat. She breathed through it. She had been breathing through it for fourteen months. She had learned to.

The collar was not on a remote.

That was the thing she had not, in fourteen months, told anyone, even Jiyad. The collar was on a remote, in the sense that there was a remote, paired to her collar, in a drawer in Director Vale’s office on the staff level, in the same way that there was a remote in a drawer for the four other collars on the four other Jedi. But the remote did not work the way the briefs said it worked. It did not switch off her connection to the Force. It did not need to. The collar emitted a small constant null pulse — not the room pulse, a smaller one — which made her Force reach unreliable inside about a metre and a half of her own skin. Outside that, the Force was fine. She could *feel* fine. She just could not *do* fine. She could not lift a cup with the Force. She could not push a man across a hallway. She could not slow her own falling heart.

She could meditate.

She could meditate, and she did, and Jiyad let her, on the long Hexani sixths when the city slowed down and the heat dropped and the cliff put its shadow across the staff yard. He let her sit in the yard. He let her sit *armed*, sometimes; he had given her back her hilt in the second month, on the agreement that the crystal was no longer in it, and she had taken the empty hilt and meditated with it across her knees because the hilt was hers and the hilt had been her grandfather’s, and Jiyad had known this from the brief, and had handed it across with the words *I don’t want a thing that isn’t yours to give back*. Then he had walked out.

Then he had come back, in the third month, with the crystal in a small pouch.

He had not put it in for her.

He had set the pouch on the bench next to her hilt, and he had said, *Don’t make me regret this*, and he had walked out again. The crystal had been in a pouch on her hip since.

She had not put it in the hilt.

The hilt had been in the empty drawer in her quarters since the third month. The crystal had been on her hip since the third month. Neither alone could do anything. Together, in her hands, with her quiet, they could do quite a lot.

She had not put them together.

She had not put them together because Jiyad had given her the crystal and asked her not to make him regret it, and Jiyad, when he gave a thing, gave it; and a Jedi did not, except under the worst of circumstances, take a man’s gift and use it against the man who had given it. *The dignity of the gift*, Master Skywalker had said in the seminar five years ago, *is one of the few clean things in a long war. Receive it well.* So she had received it well. She had received it well for eleven months. She would, possibly, go on receiving it well for a year, two, ten.

Or she would, today, with the Master of the Order’s niece in the room across the hall and the Empire of the Hand at the table, not.

She finished the flatcake. She set the wrapper in the waste bin.

She washed her hands.

She dried her hands on the towel by the basin.

She stood, for a count of four, with her eyes closed, breathing.

Then she opened her eyes and went back through the hatch toward the audience floor, because Soris had come into the galley a minute ago and told her quietly that his Lordship was on his way back from the lift, and his Lordship had asked for her at the table.

Maerko came in through the back door at twelve minutes past the hour.

The shirtsleeves were the same shirtsleeves. The coat was the same coat. But the coat had a small dark smear, low, near the right hip, of something that was either grease from the depot or somebody’s blood from the depot. Jaina, sitting at his right hand again, was close enough to smell. It was blood. Faint. The faint metallic of a smear an hour old, dried in, set into the weave. He had not changed. He had washed his hands.

He sat down. The chair complained again. He laid his forearms on the table again. He looked at the four of them, in turn, again. He stopped on T’oni.

“Senator,” he said. “Sorry. I owe you an hour. I’m gonna give you two if you’ll take ‘em. We can run past supper if you’re game.”

“We are at your disposal, Lord Maerko.”

“I appreciate that.”

“The matter,” T’oni said. Carefully. “At the depot.”

“Handled.”

“Resolved.”

“Resolving.” He took the cup. He drank. He set the cup down. “Some prick from outside the cordon. Slaver. New money. Thought he’d sneak in, lift a crate, lift some plans, lift some — yeah. The plans, frankly, I don’t care about. He took a draft schematic for a hull class we’re working on, his people will look at it for a year, they won’t make sense of it, the spec doesn’t mean anything without the keel work and we’ve got the keel. He also took twenty-six civilians on the way out. Twenty-six. From the residential cordon. Domestics, mostly. The kid who did our pad’s laundry. The chief surgeon’s niece. A woman called Pera who runs the corner shop on Forks Hill that does the only decent flatcake within twelve blocks. We’re going to get them back.”

He said this evenly. The lemon frown did not move.

“We’re going to get them back,” Maerko said, “because I’m sending a team. The team is good. The team will, probably, also get the slaver. The slaver is going to take longer to die than the civilians took to get out of the building.” He glanced, briefly, at Kyp. “That’s a — that’s a normal sentence in my Confederation, Master Durron. Just so you know. He raided me. He took *my* people. He is going to take a while to die. I’m not, like, hiding that.”

Kyp did not answer.

Maerko looked at T’oni again.

“Anyway,” he said. “The other reason I’m late is, I went down and walked the second depot myself. Not the one he hit. The other one. The bigger one.”

“The bigger one,” T’oni said.

“Yeah.”

“The one with what in it.”

Maerko looked at her.

He looked at her for a moment. Then he picked up the cup. Then he set the cup back down without drinking from it. Then he leaned, very slightly, forward, with his forearms still on the table, and he laid both his hands flat on the wood the way a man lays his hands flat on a counter to ask, evenly, for a price he hopes will be reasonable.

“Senator,” he said. “I’m gonna be straight with you, ‘cause we’ve been straight with each other so far and it’s working. So I’m gonna stay there.”

“Lord Maerko.”

“The bigger depot,” Maerko said. “The other one. The one I walked. That one’s got — yeah. That one’s got the nasties.”

T’oni waited.

Maerko shrugged. He shrugged the way a man shrugs about the weather.

“Gas,” he said. “Nerve. Six pallets of the Imperial discontinued, fenpyr-class — your office calls it CL-Six, my warehouseman calls it the green stuff, we’ve been keeping the seals tight. Two more pallets of the older Imperial — the Tarkin stockpile from before they switched chemistries; we don’t know how much of *that* still works, we’ve been afraid to test. A pair of the proton charges off the *Lusankya* logistics run, never deployed, never accounted for, still in their original cradles. Some battlefield clearance items from the Yuuzhan Vong push. And —” He flicked his fingers, lightly. “— some older bits. Some really old bits. We came up on the older bits a couple of years back. Bought a cache off a Mandalorian shipping factor whose grandfather had bought it off another factor whose grandfather had it off a clan vault that hadn’t been opened since — yeah. Since they were kicking in force, alongside the Sith. *Old* old. Stuff that was old when our great-grandfathers’ great-grandfathers were having the Clone Wars. Most of it had spoiled. Some of it hadn’t. Tibanna’s been paying well, so we’ve been — we’ve been getting the *hadn’t* back up to potency. Carefully. With proper handling. With a team.”

T’oni had gone, slightly, pale.

T’oni was very good. She kept it off her face. It went into her hands. Her right thumb pressed, hard, against the bone of her left index finger.

Jag did not move at all.

Kyp made a small, almost inaudible noise — a single soft sound through his nose.

Maerko caught the sound. He glanced sideways at Kyp. He smiled. Not really. He moved the lemon frown in the way that, on him, was as close as he came to a smile, which was barely a smile at all.

“Yeah,” Maerko said. “I know. I know how it sounds.”

“Lord Maerko,” T’oni said.

“It’s a deterrent, Senator.”

“Lord Maerko —”

“It is a *deterrent*,” Maerko said. He spread his palms. “Look. You’re a reasonable woman. I’m a reasonable man. We’re sitting at a table. We’re drinking tea. Nobody is going to use any of that. Nobody at this table wants any of that to come out of any of those crates. That is the *point*. I’ve got the crates so that I do not have to use what comes out of them. I’ve told you about the crates because I want you to know that if it ever goes the other way — if your hawks in the Alliance vote me down on the lanes and your Master of the Order decides the seventeen need a punitive expedition and your Empire — ” He nodded, politely, to Jag. “ — has its day on me on the Run, then I’ve got the crates. And the crates open. And I will, with regret, with sincere personal regret, Senator — open ‘em.”

“You will use,” T’oni said, carefully, “nerve gas. On Alliance soldiers.”

Maerko shrugged.

He shrugged the second time. The same shrug as before. *Yeah. Sure. Why are we doing this part.*

“I’d rather not,” he said. “But yeah. I’d use the gas. I’d use a charge or two, in the right place, if the right place came up. I want you to know that. I want it on the record. I want it as ugly on the record as it can be, Senator, because the uglier it is on the record, the less likely we get there. You see what I’m doing.”

“I see what you’re doing,” T’oni said.

“Good.”

“And what you are doing,” T’oni said, “is threatening to mass-murder soldiers of the Galactic Alliance with weapons your civilisation outlawed five thousand years ago.”

“Yes, Senator,” Maerko said. Evenly. “That’s what I’m doing.”

Kyp said, very softly, “Light help us.”

“Light is at the table, Master Durron,” Maerko said. He did not look at Kyp. He was still looking at T’oni. “Light is right next to you. Light just poured your senator the tea.”

He picked up his cup. He drank. He set the cup down.

“So,” he said. “The lanes.”

T’oni did not speak.

T’oni was, Jaina realised, taking a long careful breath through her nose.

Jaina caught Veya’s eye, finally — finally, after the whole hour, after the depot, after the smear of blood on the coat, after the crates. Veya had come back into the room behind Maerko and was standing now at the side of his chair, hands folded again, eyes on the middle distance again. Jaina caught her eye for the count of one. Veya did not change her face. But the Force around her, the small clean flame, flickered, briefly — not a signal, not an answer, just a brush.

*Hold. Release. Hold. Release.*

“Lord Maerko,” Jaina said.

Maerko looked at her.

“Master Solo Fel.”

“Before we do the lanes.”

“Yeah.”

“I’d like,” Jaina said, “to talk about the woman standing next to you.”

Maerko looked at Veya. He looked at Veya for a long, slow beat. Then he looked back at Jaina.

The frown did not move.

“Yeah,” he said. “Figured you would.”

He laid both forearms on the table again.

“Go on,” he said.
