# Chapter 1 — Hexani V
## Part 1 of 3

The cloud cover broke at five hundred meters and Hexani V opened beneath them, wet and gray and full of light.

Jaina pressed two fingers against the viewport. The capital sprawled along a river basin, gridded out in the unsentimental rectangles of a mining town. The rectangles were full. Cranes worked over the docks. New durasteel showed pale among older buildings, scaffolding still up on a tower near the center. Smoke from the refineries rose straight in the still air. The streets had traffic. The lights were on.

She had expected ruins.

"They've been busy," Jag said behind her.

"Mm."

Kyp Durron leaned around her shoulder to look. He'd kept his Jedi robes; the Council had argued about that for two hours and lost. "Six months ago this place was a hole."

"It's still a hole," Senator T'oni said. She didn't look up from her datapad. "It just has paint."

Jaina watched the landing pad rise toward them. The pad sat on a rooftop fifty stories above the river. Beneath the awning she could already see the welcome detail. White armor. Eleven of them. The number registered before she had consciously counted it: eleven, not the old Imperial four or eight. The new doctrine. She had read the briefing on the way out.

"Eleven," she said.

"I see them."

The pilot brought them down with the careful precision of a man who knew he was being watched by anti-aircraft systems. The pad clamps hissed. Jaina unbuckled and the others followed her up.

At the hatch she stopped. She breathed once through her nose and reached for the Force.

It was there. It came when she called it. But something in the air resisted — a faint pressure behind her eyes, like trying to read a book through smoke. She closed her grip on it and held on.

"You feel that?" Kyp said.

"Yeah."

Jag adjusted his cuff. "Bad?"

"I don't know yet."

The hatch opened.

The rain was not rain so much as the memory of rain: a wet smell, a fine wash on the deck plates, the air thick with metal dust. Jaina walked down the ramp first because it was her job to walk down the ramp first. Jag a half-step behind her on the right. Kyp on the left. T'oni and her two aides came last, the senator already arranging her face into the calm she wore when she was working.

A trooper waited at the foot of the ramp with his helmet tucked under one arm. The other hand sat on the carbine sling. The white at his elbow showed in patches; the rest of the plate had been covered in worn drab panels pressed flat over the Imperial finish, scuffed to bare metal at the edges where the scrim got most of the wear.

"Master Solo Fel."

"Sergeant."

"Master Durron. Senator."

A half-beat.

"Colonel Fel."

Jag's face did not move, which was how Jaina knew the *Yvarema* business had not been her imagination.

"This way."

The other ten formed up — four ahead, four behind, two on the flanks — at a spacing wider than Imperial drill called for. The carbines were wrong too: shorter than an E-11, fatter through the receiver, with a sighting block above the barrel that housed more than glass. Two of the troopers carried sniper variants. One had a stubby weapon with a wide muzzle and a drum magazine. The briefing had mentioned scatterguns.

Every one of them wore a flat rectangular pack between the shoulder blades. The packs hummed — low, an electric whine you only heard when you were close enough to hear someone breathe. She had heard the same hum a week ago on Coruscant, off the recorder of a captured trooper's gear. Shield generator. Pack and emitter.

One of them at the rear walked easier than the others. No shield pack between his shoulder blades. Instead a vented cylinder strapped across his chest, the length of his forearm, a bank of indicator lights down its flank, all green.

The pressure behind her eyes thickened.

Jag saw her face. "Yours?"

"His." She nodded at the trooper. "Don't look."

"Wasn't planning to."

Kyp's hand had drifted toward where his lightsaber would have been if they had been allowed to bring lightsabers. The Hexani had been polite about it. The lightsabers were back on the *Pulsar Skate* under guard by Jaina's own people. *Polite,* the briefing had said. *Don't be insulted. Be relieved.*

The squad walked them off the pad and into a covered ramp that led down into the building. The corridor was wide and the ceilings were high — built for cargo, repurposed for governance. The walls were the kind of gray that doesn't bother with itself. The lighting fixtures were new. The doors were new. A maintenance droid moved past them on a side track, dragging a bucket on wheels. The droid was an ancient MSE-6 repainted with a fresh sigil: a six-pointed star inside an open hand.

"Cute," T'oni said.

"They polish them," Kyp said.

"They polish everything."

A turbolift took them down ten floors. The trooper next to Jaina in the lift was a woman by the line of her hips and the way she stood; the helmet showed nothing. She did not look at Jaina. None of them did. They had none of the old Imperial tic of stealing glances at Jedi.

The doors opened into a long anteroom with a long table and chairs along the walls. Not a throne room. Not a war room either. Aides moved through it carrying flimsi and datapads. A clerk at a side desk was arguing with someone over a comlink about a shipment of bacta. A protocol droid stood by the far door with its hands clasped in front of it like a butler.

The sergeant gestured.

"He'll see you now."

He did not say *Lord Maerko*. He did not say *the Director*. He said *he.* The pronoun did the work.

Jaina stepped through the door.

---

The office had been a conference room. Someone had dragged something heavy across the long table once and not bothered to refinish the scar. Three of the chairs had been swapped out for armchairs. The wall behind the desk wasn't a wall; it was a window, and the desk faced the window instead of the door. So did the man behind the desk.

He didn't stand.

"Master Solo Fel. Welcome to Hexani V. Sorry about the rain. Please sit."

She sat. The others followed.

The hookah at his right elbow was already lit. The room smelled of apple tobacco and tea and faintly of gun oil. He had rolled his sleeves past the elbow some time before the meeting, and the forearms below the rolled cloth did not come from sitting at a desk. The shirt was olive drab and unmarked. No rank. No flash. He spoke with the round vowels of an Outer Rim enlisted accent.

"Thank you for coming. I know it's a long way."

"Lord Maerko."

He looked at her.

He kept looking. Long enough to be a question. Long enough for the question to land in the room.

She caught the shape of the thought before he closed it off — not the words, the shape — through the haze of whatever that trooper had carried in the hall.

*What kind of title is that.*

"It's what your aides call you," she said.

"Yeah." He scratched his jaw. "They like it. I don't mind it. You don't have to."

"What should I call you."

"Jiyad. Or sir. Sir's easier on you, I think."

"Jiyad."

"There you go."

He looked at the others. He nodded at Jag. "Colonel. Your father send his regards?"

"He did."

"Tell him I haven't forgotten the *Yvarema* business. We'll talk about it later if there's time."

Jag's face did not move. "Yes."

"Master Durron." Maerko's eyes hadn't left Jag's face for a half-second after the *Yvarema*. He let the silence work. Then he turned. "I read your dissertation."

Kyp blinked. "I didn't write a dissertation."

"The Carida thing. The chapter on you. I read that. Not for fun. They made me read it at officer course." He picked up a stylus and turned it between his fingers. "I have questions, if we end up with the time."

"All right."

"Senator T'oni."

T'oni inclined her head with the precision of a woman who had been inclining her head professionally for forty years. "Director Maerko."

"That's a new one."

"I won't make a habit of it."

"Appreciate it." He set the stylus down. "Tea?"

He didn't wait for an answer. He turned in his chair and called over his shoulder, not loud.

"Talia."

A side door opened.

The Force went thin around her before she was three steps in — not gone, throttled — and Jaina's eyes went to the source: a flat metal band at her throat, two fingers wide, a small green light pulsing once on the side and steadying. The band sat above the collar of a sleeveless gray tunic. The tunic was the kind of clothing you put on someone you have decided not to dress.

A Jedi.

Jaina did not look at Kyp. She knew what Kyp's face was doing.

The woman — Talia, Maerko had said — set the tray down without rattling the cups. She poured. Her hand was steady. She knew the order: Jaina first, then Jag, then Kyp, then the senator. She did not pour for Maerko because Maerko had not asked. She stepped back behind his chair and put her hands at the small of her back.

She did not look at the visitors.

Maerko picked up his own cup. "Tarine. Good for you. Decaffeinated. I'm trying to cut down."

Kyp set his cup down without drinking.

"Suit yourself," Maerko said, and drank.

T'oni broke first, because that was what senators did. "Director Maerko, perhaps we could begin with—"

"Sure." He set his cup down. "What do you want."

T'oni paused half a breath. "We were hoping to hear your terms."

"You came all the way out here to ask me my terms."

"Yes."

"That was lazy of you."

The senator's smile did not change. "Director?"

"Your intel section's been sitting on me for eight months. Half my comm traffic goes through people you turned. I know which of my line commanders you've offered money to. I know which ones took it. I know what you think you know. You came out here to ask my terms? You came out here to look at me. Which is fine. That's a thing diplomats do. I'd just rather we be honest about what we're actually doing in this room before we start telling each other lies."

T'oni's smile held. Jaina watched the muscles work to hold it.

"You want my terms," Maerko said. "Fine. We'll do it your way."

He leaned back. The hookah hose came up into his hand like it lived there. He drew on it. He let the smoke out slowly, sideways, away from his guests, with the absent courtesy of a man who lived with smokers.

"The Salin Corridor," he said. "Lane traffic and customs. I want the Confederation written into the registry as a recognized signatory. Same standing as Bothan Space, Hapan, whoever else you let on the manifest these days. I want the Perlemian opened from Esfandia out to the Argai cluster — that's about thirty parsecs of lane I'm running blind on. And the eastern stretch of the Hydian, from Onadax down to where it meets the Salin. Customs reciprocity. Toll-sharing. I'll pay my share. I want a seat at the lane board. I want my flag on the buoys."

T'oni had been taking notes. She stopped taking notes.

"Anything else."

"Yeah. The seventeen systems are mine. They stay mine. You can call them what you like in your speeches, but they don't come back, they don't get a referendum, and they don't get visited by your Jedi unless I invite them. Which I won't."

Kyp opened his mouth.

"Don't," Maerko said, without looking at him. "Master Durron. Not yet. I'll get to you."

Kyp shut his mouth.

"The stormtrooper program stays. I don't care what the Senate's subcommittee on militarized identity has decided. Eat a dick. I was a trooper. I'm still a trooper. The boys outside are troopers. We're keeping the white. We're keeping the rank structure. We're keeping the training. If you want to debate it, you debate it with someone else, because that's not a thing I'm going to discuss."

T'oni made a small mark on her datapad.

"And the prisoners are mine."

That one came out quieter.

"They're prisoners of war," T'oni said. "There are conventions."

"Yeah. We'll come back to that."

A silence.

He picked up his cup again. He drank. He set it down.

"That's what I want. Tell me what you want, and we'll see if there's a meeting point. If there isn't, you go home, and we keep doing what we've been doing, and in six months you can come back and ask me again, and I'll have moved the line. So I'd take the meeting today, if I were you. But you do you."

---

Jaina drank her tea. It was good tea.

She set the cup down. "The prisoners."

"What about them."

"You have eleven of ours."

"I have nine of yours. The other two were Imperial Knights, which I assume the colonel here is more interested in than you are. Different conversation."

"Nine, then. We want them back."

"I figured."

"What do you want for them."

He considered her. The frown softened, just at the edges, into something that wasn't quite a smile.

"One system per Jedi."

She did not laugh. It was a close thing.

"No."

"No?"

"No."

"What's a Jedi worth to you these days?"

"Less than a system."

"Master Solo Fel. That's cold."

"It's the truth."

"I like the truth." He looked at Talia, casual, like he was checking the time off a wall clock. "Hear that? She's a hardliner. We can work with hardliners."

Talia did not respond. Her hands stayed clasped behind her back.

Kyp's voice came out tight. "Where did you get her."

Maerko looked at Kyp for the first time properly. He looked at him for a long moment. Then he picked up the hookah hose again.

"Battle of Kallos Station," he said. "Eight months ago. She was the last one standing in a corridor of dead troopers. About forty of them, give or take. I was watching the helmet cams in real time from the carrier. She did a thing with her saber where she spun through five of them in one move and held the pose at the end like she was waiting for an applause sign. I clipped that. I keep it on a loop. I'll show you, if we get time."

"How."

"She was tired. She had a saber cut down the side of her leg. We dropped a null array on the corridor and put six scatterguns on her at the doorway and she lowered her weapon. Smart kid. I'd have done the same."

"You collared her."

"Yes."

"That's a slave collar."

"It's an inhibitor. The slave part is editorial." He drew on the hookah. "She's a prisoner of war. She got the same offer everyone else got. Six months of cooperative behavior, she comes off the collar, she gets a parole status. She turned it down. So she's still wearing the collar. That's a choice she's making, not one I'm making."

Talia spoke for the first time.

"That's a lie."

Her voice was flat and dry.

Maerko's mouth twitched.

"It's *partly* a lie," he said. "The parole offer's real. The six months is real. I'd let her out tomorrow. The collar's still on her because she keeps lifting cutlery."

"I have not lifted cutlery."

"You lifted a paring knife last Tuesday."

"I lifted a paring knife into a *drawer*. I was *putting it away.*"

"You lifted it with your mind, Talia."

"I have hands."

"You used the Force on the cutlery."

"It was a *paring knife*, you absolute—"

"Tea."

"What."

"More tea. The senator's cup is low."

She looked at him. She did not move.

"Please," he added, after a beat.

She moved. She refilled T'oni's cup, then Jag's, then Jaina's. She did not refill Kyp's, because Kyp had not touched his. She did not refill her master's, because her master had not asked. She stepped back behind his chair.

Jaina caught Kyp's eye. Kyp looked at her like she had just asked him to swallow a fork.

"Director," T'oni said carefully. "The collar."

"What about it."

"Removable, you said."

"Today, if she takes the parole."

"Without the parole."

"Field-removable by an engineer. Three minutes with the right code key. Yes."

"And the engineers."

"On staff."

"On staff *here.*"

"Yes."

T'oni made another note. Jaina watched her make it.

The senator put her stylus down.

"Director, I will be direct with you. The Knight is a non-starter."

"As what."

"As a hostage in this conversation. We will not negotiate over a collared Jedi sitting in the room. If we are negotiating for her release, she leaves the room. If we are negotiating for the prisoners as a class, she leaves the room. If we are negotiating for anything else, she leaves the room. This isn't a moral position. It's a procedural one."

Maerko thought about it. He turned his head, lazy, to look at Talia.

"You want to leave?"

"No."

"There you are."

"Director—"

"She's staff. She's not a hostage. She's pouring tea. She's allowed to stay. If you'd rather not negotiate in front of her, you can leave, and I'll send you a polite note tomorrow telling you what I'm taking next."

T'oni breathed in through her nose.

She picked her stylus back up.

---

Jaina set her cup down. "The lanes."

"What about them."

"Salin, Perlemian eastern stretch, Hydian eastern stretch. That's a lot of map."

"It is."

"Why those three."

He smiled. It was small. It did not reach his eyes. It did something else with his face, something private and not unfriendly.

"Because that's how the boys in Section Three eat," he said. "I have seventeen systems. They produce a lot of things. Tibanna gas, mostly. Refined metals. Carbon ore. Pharma precursors out of Marsteen. None of it gets to market if I don't have lane access. The Alliance has been quietly leaning on the lane authorities to slow my customs paperwork. Average wait at Esfandia is now nine days for a Hexani-flagged hauler. Bothan ships, twelve hours. That's not random."

T'oni did not deny it.

"So I want it formalized. I want my flag on the buoys. I want my customs officers in the booths. I want the wait times equalized. In exchange, I'll pay tariffs. I'll honor warrants for non-political criminals — actual smugglers, actual pirates, actual slavers. I'll let your inspectors aboard my ships under reciprocity. I'm not asking for charity. I'm asking to be a country."

Kyp said, quietly, "You are not a country."

Maerko looked at him.

"I have seventeen systems," he said. "I have an army. I have a fleet. I have a treasury. I have a flag. I have an anthem, which is terrible, but I have one. I have a hospital system. I have a court. I have a customs office. What word would you use, Master Durron."

"Warlord."

"Warlord's a job. It's not a country."

"Then call yourself a warlord."

"I do." He shrugged. "Warlord with a country. It's not exclusive."

T'oni cut in. "The Alliance recognizes territorial integrity through certain procedural mechanisms. Recognition isn't a thing the Alliance can grant in a room. It would require—"

"Hearings. Yes. Subcommittees. Yes. A vote in the General Assembly. Yes. I have read the procedure. I would like the procedure to start. That's why I'm willing to give you something."

"What."

"A Knight."

Talia made a soft sound behind him.

"Not her," Maerko said, without turning. "Calm down."

Talia's hands unclenched at the small of her back.

"There's a Padawan we picked up four weeks ago at Mertis. Voss. Fourteen, fifteen, somewhere in there. She's been polite. She has not lifted cutlery. We don't have a collar that fits her. I'd like to give her back."

T'oni blinked once.

"In exchange for what."

"Nothing. Goodwill gesture. You take her home. She tells her side. The holos make a thing of it. Somebody in your Senate gets to look magnanimous and not stupid. It costs me a hangar bay and a shuttle. It costs you the trouble of saying thank you."

"And the catch."

"You start the hearings. I don't need a vote. I just need you to file the paperwork. The vote can take a year. I have a year."

T'oni made a careful note.

Jag spoke for the first time in twenty minutes. "Director."

"Colonel."

"The shield generators."

"What about them."

"Your sergeant outside is wearing one. Standard issue?"

"Every regular trooper. Yes."

"Domestic production?"

A pause. Maerko's mouth did the not-smile thing again.

"Yes."

"Where."

"Not telling you that, Colonel."

"I had to ask."

"You did. Asked and answered."

Jaina watched Jag's face. Jag had asked, she suspected, not because he expected an answer but because he had wanted Maerko to refuse the answer in a particular way. The way Maerko had refused told Jag something. Jaina could not yet tell what.

She filed it.

"Director." T'oni again. "On the question of the prisoner program. We have a delegation prepared to discuss conditions, inspections—"

"Sure. Send them. Section Six handles that. Director Vale. She's good. She'll meet with you for as long as you want."

"And the auxiliaries?"

The room cooled.

Maerko picked up his cup. He set it down without drinking.

"What about them."

"There are reports," T'oni said carefully, "of compelled service. Of stimulant use. Of explosive vests."

"All true."

"Director—"

"All true," he said again. "I'm not going to dance with you on that one. I have a program. I use criminals. I drug them. I send them at minefields. I send them at fortified positions. I send them at boarding actions. They are pirates and slavers and contract killers, mostly. They were going to be executed under their own systems' laws. I gave them a six-month service offer instead. Some of them survive. The ones who survive get a stake at a parole farm on Drun. The ones who don't, don't. I am aware of how it looks. I do not require you to like it. I am not, however, going to pretend it isn't happening, because I think pretending is the worst thing I could do to those men."

Silence.

"Next question," he said.

T'oni picked up her stylus and put it back down.

"Director Vale," she said. "Will see us."

"She will."

---

Maerko shifted in his chair. The chair creaked. He looked at his hookah, picked up the hose, drew. The coal in the bowl had gone gray. He frowned at the bowl.

"Eshan."

A technician at a side console looked up. Bald. Clean coverall.

"Sir."

"Coal."

"Right."

Eshan got up. He came over with a small pair of tongs and replaced the coal on the bowl. While he did it Maerko was looking at Jaina.

"I read your file," he said.

"You said."

"I said his." He nodded at Kyp. "I didn't say yours. Yours I read on my own. Twelve different briefs. Six biographies. The monograph your aunt wrote on Force healing. I have a folder."

"That's flattering."

"It's not flattering, it's homework. I read the people I'm going to be sitting across a desk from. You showed up with three. I read all three. The biographies on the senator are better than the ones on you. They're funnier."

T'oni's smile, which had been holding all afternoon, briefly cracked into a real one.

"Tell me what I got wrong," Maerko said. "About you. In the brief."

Jaina considered him.

"I don't know what was in the brief."

"Best guess."

"That I'm angry."

"Are you."

"Yes."

"At me?"

"At the situation."

"Mm." He looked at the hookah. The new coal was warming. "That's a politician's answer."

"It's also a true one."

"It can be both."

She drank. She set the cup down. "You executed the governor's family on Margeal."

He looked at her.

"Yes."

"You drowned the son."

"Yes."

"In front of his mother."

"And then I killed the mother. And then I killed the father. Yes."

"Why."

He thought about it.

"Because the alternative was a six-month insurgency that would have killed twenty thousand people on a planet of two million, and the governor's family was the engine of it, and I judged the trade. I'm not going to pretend it was clean. It wasn't. I did it on camera. I did it on purpose. The next two governors I talked to surrendered in an afternoon. The trade worked."

"Don't use that word."

He blinked. "Trade?"

"Math. You were about to. Don't."

He held her eyes. He sat with that for a long beat. Eshan, the bald technician, had backed away from the hookah and was pretending to look at his console. Talia, behind Maerko's chair, had gone very still.

Finally Maerko nodded. Not in agreement. Acknowledgment.

"Fine. Not in this room."

He picked up his cup. He saw it was empty. He looked at it like the cup had betrayed him personally.

"Talia."

"Pour your own."

"Talia."

"I am not pouring while you are doing a war crime confession, Jiyad. That's gauche."

"It wasn't a confession, I was *answering a question*—"

"You were *bragging*."

"I was answering a—"

"You looked at her like a man who wanted credit—"

"I did not—"

"—for being honest about it, which, I would point out—"

"Talia—"

"—is not the same thing as *being* honest about it, because honest people do not perform their—"

"*Talia.*"

She stopped. She tilted her head. She did the smallest, driest impression of him stuttering through his own next word.

It was perfect.

Jaina felt her mouth move before she could control it. Not a smile. The shape of one. She pressed her teeth into her lower lip until the shape went away.

Maerko opened his mouth.

He closed it.

He pointed at Talia, then at the door, then back at Talia, then at the door again. The gesture was clearly meant to convey a finished thought, and the thought was not finished. The technician — Eshan — turned away to face the wall, his shoulders moving in a way that was definitely not professional posture.

Maerko caught it.

"Stop laughing."

"I'm not laughing, sir."

"You're laughing. You're laughing at me."

"No, sir."

"You're not even a person, Eshan. You're a testicle."

Jag, in the act of swallowing tea, made a sound. He set the cup down very carefully on the table. He coughed once into his fist. The cough became a longer cough. It was, technically, not laughter. It was as close as Jagged Fel had been to laughter in Jaina's presence in three years of marriage.

"That's because I shave my head, sir," Eshan said.

"That's why I said it, you bald fucking—"

"Director Maerko."

A new voice. From the side door. Quiet, urgent.

Maerko shut his mouth.

A young woman in Section Two gray waited at the door with a flimsi held flat against her thigh.

"Excuse me," Maerko said. He stood up — he wasn't tall standing — and crossed to her in three steps. She murmured. He bent his head to hers.

Jaina watched.

She let the Force out the way she had been taught to let it out under the array's pressure: not pushing, not pulling, just opening her ears.

She caught the whisper in pieces.

"...Enixon side, last night..."

"...the depot?"

"...and the civvies. Twelve, fourteen of them. They took six."

"...what else..."

"...the *Bellator* plans, sir."

A pause.

"All of them?"

"The hull schematics. The reactor block. We don't know about the keel package yet, we're still confirming, but at least the hull and the reactor."

A longer pause.

When Maerko spoke again his voice had a different quality. Not louder. Lower. The frown had stopped being a habit.

"Who."

"Sshoa'qhi the Hutt's bagman. They think. We're confirming."

"Get me Section Two in twenty minutes. Full table. Boros. Toll on lift. I want a team put together. Real one. Not the second string. I want him hunted down. I want him face-down on a deck plate inside ten days."

"Yes, sir."

He started to turn back, then turned back to her.

"And I want them recording."

"Sir?"

"When they take him. I want a recorder running. I want to see the face. I want to hear him scream. Cut off his ballsack and feed it to him on a plate. I want it on the recording. He pillaged civilians. I am not in a charitable mood."

"Yes, sir."

"Go."

She went.

He stood for a moment looking at the door she had gone through. He put his hand against the frame. He pressed against it once, the way you press a sore tooth with your tongue.

Then he walked back to the chair and sat down.

He picked up the hookah hose.

He drew on it.

He put it down.

Talia said, mildly, "Castration's a war crime."

He looked up at her.

"You were listening."

"You were stage-whispering. The senator's astromech could have heard you."

"Stop listening."

"I am standing two feet behind your head."

"Talia."

"You're going to do it anyway."

"I am, in fact, going to do it anyway."

"Then don't pretend it bothers you when I hear about it."

"It does not bother me. It bothers the senator. Look at the senator."

Jaina did not look at the senator.

"Talia."

"Jiyad."

"If you don't stop, I'm going to put you back in the kitchen."

"If you do that, I will piss in your teacup next time."

"You—"

"I will. I will piss in your teacup, and I will smile while I am doing it, and you will not be able to tell, because you do not pay attention to the things you put in your mouth."

A beat.

"Out."

She left at her own pace. She walked past Jaina close enough that Jaina could feel the small radius of muted Force around her — the throttle on the collar, the green light pulsing once as she passed. Talia did not look at her. But as she went through the side door she lifted one hand briefly behind her own shoulder, in the shape of a sign Jaina had not seen in months: not a code. A greeting. *I see you.*

Jaina did not return it.

The door closed.

Maerko sat back. He looked at his guests.

The not-smile thing did its work and went away.

"Sorry," he said. "Where were we."

"You were giving us terms," T'oni said.

"Right."

He picked up the stylus. He set it down. He looked at the table for a while. Then he looked back up.

"There are going to be more interruptions like that today. I have a man on my eastern border who has just done something stupid. I am about to spend the next eight hours making him regret it. So if it's all right with you, I'd like to move this room to the back office. Smaller. Quieter. The senator can have a chair with arms. The Jedi can pretend the smell doesn't bother them. We can keep talking, and I can run my hunt at the same time. Yes?"

T'oni looked at Jaina. Jaina looked at Jag. Jag inclined his head a half-degree. Not yes. *Permission to consider.*

Kyp had not spoken in ten minutes. He spoke now.

"Director."

"Master Durron."

"Before we move."

"Yes."

"The Knight."

"What about her."

"You don't intend to give her up."

"No."

"Not for a system."

"Not for three."

"Why."

He thought about it. He took longer to think about it than he had taken to think about anything else in the conversation.

"Because she is not the worst thing I have done," he said, "and she is the one I would like to do least badly. That's the closest I can give you to honest. I am not letting her go because I do not know how to let her go without making it worse. If that's a problem for the Senate, the Senate can negotiate around it. If it's a problem for the Order, the Order will have to live with it. I'm not arguing about it today."

He stood up.

"Back office. Five minutes. Aides can wait in the anteroom. There's caf. Master Durron, if you want to walk for a minute, I'd recommend the south balcony. It's the one with the view that isn't on fire."

He walked past them toward the side door Talia had gone through.

At the door he stopped. He turned around.

"Master Solo Fel."

"Yes."

"The pronunciation. Solo Fel. The Fel's not silent, is it."

"It is not."

"Got it. Thanks."

He went through the door.

It clicked shut behind him.

For a moment nobody moved.

Then Kyp let out a breath he had been holding for a long time, very slowly, through his teeth.

T'oni began composing a message on her datapad. Her hands were steady. Her face was working. She did not look up.

Jag set his cup down carefully in the saucer.

"He builds his own shields," he said.

"What."

"Domestic production. He confirmed it by refusing to say where. If it were an import, he'd have lied about it. He builds them. That changes the brief."

"How many."

"That's the next question."

Outside the window, on the river, a barge hauled refined metal downstream. The cranes worked. The refineries smoked. A wedge of birds — actual birds, somehow, in this air — crossed the gray over the docks and was gone.

Jaina pressed her fingers against the bridge of her nose. The pressure of the array sat just behind her eyes, low and constant, like the start of a headache. She let it sit.

Kyp said, "Jaina."

"Mm."

"She was watching you. The whole time. Not him."

Jaina lifted her head.

"I know."

"She wanted you to know it."

"I know that too."

Kyp's mouth pulled. "We came here for the lanes."

"I know what we came here for, Kyp."

"Then don't."

"Don't what."

"Don't take her back as the thing that matters."

Jaina looked at the side door. The light was off above it now. Wherever Talia had gone, it was somewhere on the other side of the wall, and the wall was thick.

"It already matters," she said.

T'oni's stylus paused over the datapad.

The senator did not lift her head. She finished her sentence. She tapped *send.*

The side door opened. The other one. Not Maerko's.

A man came in carrying a tray of water glasses. Section gray, unmarked. Not the aide who had pulled Maerko out.

"With the director's compliments." He set the tray down. He did not look at the cups of tea, which had gone cold.

"Thank you," T'oni said.

"There is a balcony through the door to your left. The director said one of you might want air. The next room is ten minutes, not five. He is on a call with Section Three. He sends his apologies for the delay."

"Of course."

"Is there anything else."

"No."

"There is caf in the anteroom if your aides require it. There is a comm console for outgoing messages, with the understanding that traffic from this room is monitored."

"We understand."

He bowed his head a fraction and left.

Kyp stood up. "Balcony."

"Go," Jaina said.

He went.

Jag did not get up. He stayed in his chair. He picked up his cup and looked into it. He set it down. He took a folded slip of flimsi from his cuff — Jaina had not seen him put one there — looked at it for a half-second, folded it again, put it back.

"Jag."

"Mm."

"What was that."

"Later."

He did not look at her. He turned the abandoned stylus between his fingers, the way Maerko had turned his stylus. He set it down. Then he stood up and walked to the window.

She watched her husband stand at the window and look down at the cranes. He had the set in his shoulders he had on a parade ground. He was not resting. He was reading.

She let him read.

She stayed in the chair.

After a moment she closed her eyes.

The pressure of the array was steady. It did not get worse and it did not get better. It sat. She did not push against it. She sent her attention out around the edges of it, the way she had been taught at fourteen by an old man who had spent his last winters on Dagobah.

She felt the building.

Not all of it. The array was too thick at the center, and what came back was uneven and full of false reads. But the edges came through. There were many people in this building. There were many more people in the buildings around it. There were shielded packs throughout the perimeter — she could feel them by their absence, the small empty places where troopers walked. She could feel, distant, north along the river, a colder spot, broad and clean, that meant another array. Larger than the cylinder the trooper in the hall had carried.

She could feel two other Jedi.

Two.

Not one.

One was Talia, somewhere in the building, dim and close. The other was much farther down. Several floors below the river. The other was awake and afraid.

She opened her eyes.

She did not say anything yet.

She watched Jag's back at the window. She watched T'oni finish her message and tap *send.* She heard Kyp on the balcony, his boots on the durasteel grating, then the slow creak of him leaning his weight against the railing.

She put her hands flat on the table. She felt the wood. The wood was real wood, dark, scarred. Old hardwood, salvaged from somewhere — not Hexani V's planted trees, which were forty years too young. Somewhere imported. Somewhere expensive. Or somewhere conquered.

She filed that too.

"T'oni."

The senator looked up.

"Two of them," Jaina said.

A beat.

T'oni did not ask *two of what.* T'oni had been doing this for forty years.

"You're certain."

"Below the river. Awake."

"On the prisoner list?"

"I don't have the list memorized."

"I do." T'oni's stylus moved. "Knight Vehn we knew about. Padawan Voss he just offered us. The other two were the Imperial Knights — Jag's people. That's four. If you're feeling a fifth—"

"I am."

"—then he hasn't told us about a fifth."

"Or the brief was wrong."

"Or the brief was wrong," T'oni agreed. Her face did not move. Her fingers did. "I'll have it run."

Jag turned from the window.

"Quietly," he said.

"Naturally."

Kyp came back in from the balcony. The smell of refinery smoke had come in with him on the sleeves of his robe.

"Five minutes," T'oni said, to no one in particular. "Then we go in."

"For what," Kyp said.

Jaina looked at him.

"For more," she said.
