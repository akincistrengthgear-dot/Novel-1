# Chapter 1 — The Audience at Hexani V

*Part 1 of 3*

---

The shuttle rocked through the outer skin of Hexani V's atmosphere, and Jaina Solo Fel watched her tea slosh against the inside of its cup.

The cup was Imperial issue. Black ceramic, weighted at the base, designed not to tip in turbulence. The tea inside it tipped anyway.

"The pilot's good," Jag said from the bench across the cabin. "He's flying it manual."

"I noticed."

"Showing off, do you think?"

"Maybe." Jaina set the cup back into its gimbal. "Maybe he doesn't trust the autopilot."

Jag's mouth flickered. Not a smile. Jag did not really smile in the field. But there was the suggestion of one — the kind she had learned to read in the second year of marriage, when she had finally stopped expecting Corellian theatrics and started catching the quieter Imperial vocabulary of his face.

She turned to the viewport.

The world below them was the colour of old iron. A high cold plateau, raked by wind, marked in long parallel lines that looked at first like erosion and on second look like roads. Hexani V had been a backwater six years ago. The Hexani Confederation had begun life as a single garrison on a single mining moon, and the moon had been notable for nothing except a quantity of tibanna gas trapped under its mantle and the fact that nobody had thought to count it. The reports she had read on the ride out from Bastion were thick with maps, and the maps had been redrawn three times since she packed them.

Now there were cities down there. Not great ones, not Coruscant or Hapes Prime — there were no spires of glass and no fountains of polished durasteel — but cities all the same. Functional grids. Rail lines that ran straight to mine heads and straight back again. Refinery stacks pumping pale grey smoke into a sky the colour of dishwater. A dam, an enormous one, holding back a reservoir on the eastern edge of the largest urban grid. The dam had not existed eighteen months ago. She had seen the survey holos.

"They built that fast," she said.

"They build everything fast," Jag answered. "It's a feature of the regime."

Senator G'Mai T'oni made a soft, considering sound from the forward bench. The Bothan senator had not spoken since they cleared the system's outer ring, and she had spent the silence watching the slow rotation of a datapad in her lap. T'oni was small even by Bothan standards, narrow-shouldered, her fur a pale dusty cream that the cabin lights flattered. The fur on her throat was very still.

"They build it where it pays," she said. "Look at the rail. Look where the lines go."

Jaina looked. The rail led from the refinery to the spaceport, from the spaceport to the harbour, from the harbour to the refinery again. The pattern was almost insulting in its honesty.

"He's not pretending it's a country," T'oni went on. "He's pretending it's a factory. A factory that pays its workers and feeds them and keeps the lights on. The flag is incidental."

"He has a flag?" Jaina asked.

"Six concentric hexagons. Black on grey. Nothing on it. No motto, no sun, no fist. Nothing."

"That's not a flag," Jaina said. "That's a header on a docket."

"Yes."

The cabin rocked again. The pilot was descending in long shallow curves, bleeding speed against the upper atmosphere instead of burning it off with retro-thrust. A Corellian habit. Jaina caught herself noting that and put the observation aside, because she had been told twice on the trip out that she should not catalogue these people. She should listen to them. She should not file them.

She filed the pilot anyway.

Corran Horn shifted on the bench beside her. He had been still for so long she had almost forgotten him, which was, she suspected, the point. Corran was nearer sixty than fifty now and he had grown into a stillness that other Jedi only learned in meditation. He wore his beard short and grey, his Jedi robes a dark unbleached brown, and the lightsaber at his hip was tied with the same battered leather thong he had used since the Yuuzhan Vong war.

"How close are we to ground?" he asked.

"Eight minutes," Jaina said.

"Good." He closed his eyes again. "Tell me if anything changes."

She watched him for another moment, then turned back to the viewport. Corran had asked her three times since they cleared hyperspace to tell him if anything changed. She knew what he was asking about. He was asking about the Force.

The Force on Hexani V was wrong.

It was not absent, the way it would be in a nest of ysalamiri. It was not even diminished, the way it was inside a Yuuzhan Vong vessel. It was *off-key*. Like a chord struck on an instrument tuned a quarter step flat. She had felt it the moment they came out of hyperspace, and it had been getting steadier as they descended. Not stronger — steadier. As though somebody were sitting at a console down there, holding their thumb on a control, and the control was a rotting tooth in the back of her jaw.

She had not mentioned it to Jag. There was no point. Jag could not feel it. Jag would only feel responsible for it, because Jag was the kind of man who felt responsible for weather.

She mentioned it now, quietly, to Corran. "Master."

He opened one eye.

"It's still there."

"Mm."

"Is it worse?"

"It's the same. It's an array. It's not going to be worse until we land."

"What does it do, exactly?"

"It lies to you."

Jaina waited.

"You reach for the Force," Corran said, "and the Force answers, only the answer is wrong. A push goes left instead of forward. A danger sense ticks behind you when the thing is in front of you. Your sense of time stretches. It's small but it's everywhere." He closed the eye again. "You can fight through it. You shouldn't. The point is that you don't know how badly it's lying, and so you can't trust anything you do."

"You've felt it before."

"I felt the prototype at Kallos Station," he said.

The name fell into the cabin and lay there.

Vesi Tellan had been at Kallos Station.

Jaina had not been her master. She had not known her well. Vesi had been a Knight only a year, a slender Mirialan with the long bones and the long temper that Mirialans tended to grow up into, and she had been on detached duty with a strike group when the Hexani had taken the station from inside. The strike group had been almost wiped. Vesi had been listed as killed in action for six months before the holo footage surfaced. The holo had shown her alive, behind a man with a frown and a hookah, holding a tray.

Jaina had watched the holo six times before Jag had taken the datapad out of her hand and put it face-down on the table.

"Eight minutes," the pilot said over the intercom, in Basic, calm and bored. "Strap in if you haven't. Touchdown will be soft."

T'oni reached up and pulled her harness tighter without looking. Jag did the same. Corran did not move, because Corran was already strapped. Jaina ran her thumb along the buckle of her own harness and then made herself stop.

"Jaina," Jag said.

"I'm fine."

"I didn't ask."

"I know." She put her hand flat on his knee. "I'm fine."

He turned his hand over under hers and laid two fingers against the inside of her wrist, where the pulse was, and held them there a moment, the way he sometimes did in the dark when he wanted to check that she was still there without admitting that he was checking. Then he let go.

"Remember," he said, "he wants this meeting."

"Does he."

"He asked for it."

"He asked the Alliance for it," she said. "He asked the Remnant for nothing."

"He sent the request through me."

"He sent the request through you because he knew I'd come if you carried it."

Jag did not answer that. He didn't need to.

The shuttle banked. The plateau slid up the viewport and resolved into a landing field — a long flat tarmac of fused volcanic glass, ringed by squat hangars, and at the far end a low complex of grey duracrete buildings with no windows on the side that faced the field. There was no honour guard arrayed on the apron. There was no banner. There were three flight crews servicing what looked like a row of light interceptors of a make Jaina did not immediately recognise, and a small group of armoured figures standing at parade rest near where the shuttle would set down.

The armoured figures were stormtroopers. They were not stormtroopers like any stormtroopers Jaina had ever fought.

She leaned closer to the viewport.

"Master," she said.

Corran opened both eyes.

"Look at them."

He looked.

There were eight of them in the receiving party, drawn up in a shallow arc. Their armour was the right shape — the high domed helmet, the chest cuirass, the segmented pauldrons that gave a stormtrooper his characteristic silhouette. But the colour was wrong. The traditional blinding white had been overpainted, every plate, in a flat grey-green wash that broke up the geometry of the body. Webbing crossed the chests in slabs of canvas pockets. Each man carried something on his back that Jaina had at first taken to be an oversized power pack, and was not. It was a generator. She could see the cooling fins. The thing strapped to its top plate, riding in a kind of cradle, was the size and shape of a small thermal detonator and unmistakably a personal shield emitter.

"Shields," she said.

"Standard issue, by the look of it," Corran said. "Every trooper, not just the heavy."

"That's expensive."

"Yes."

The weapons were worse. The weapons were not E-11s.

She did not have a name for what they were carrying. The receiver was longer than an E-11, the barrel shorter, the butt squared and folded down against the shoulder so the man could carry it slung across the chest without it printing through his armour. There was an optic mounted forward of the receiver, blocky and quad-lensed; she could not tell at this range whether it was a scope or a sensor suite or both. Every trooper had one, slung at the same angle, in the same place.

"That's not the carbine variant," Jag said softly. "That's something new."

"They were always going to develop something new," T'oni said. "Don't pretend to be surprised, Colonel."

Jag did not reply. He had been Colonel and a great many other things since, and T'oni knew it; she had used the rank to needle him because she suspected, correctly, that he was easier to read when irritated.

The shuttle settled. There was a small soft hiss as the landing struts compressed, a longer hiss as the engines cycled down, and then the cabin lights came up half a stop and the ramp at the rear of the cabin began to lower. Cold air came up the ramp first, smelling of fused volcanic glass and burnt ozone and, faintly, of something Jaina could not place — a kind of sweetness, like a confectioner's shop two streets away.

Tibanna, she realised. The whole atmosphere here tasted very faintly of tibanna gas. They were standing on top of an ocean of it.

She rose.

Jag rose beside her. T'oni rose. Corran rose last, settling his robe with an old soldier's economy, and the four of them turned and walked together down the ramp into Hexani V.

The cold hit her face first, and then the wind, which carried grit. The grit was fine, almost a dust, and the sky above the field was the colour of a Coruscant winter without the warmth of Coruscant in it. She squinted, took two more steps, and stopped at the foot of the ramp on the line a junior protocol officer had marked there in chalk.

The arc of stormtroopers did not move. The squad leader at the centre of the arc came forward four paces, halted at a distance that was just slightly closer than diplomatic protocol allowed, and removed his helmet.

He was a young man. Younger than Jaina, which was rare in her life now. He had a soldier's cropped haircut growing out and a sunburn across the bridge of his nose and the kind of pale eyes that came of a long line of cold-world ancestors. His expression was perfectly neutral.

"Princess Solo Fel," he said. "Senator T'oni. Master Horn. Head of State Fel. Welcome to Hexani V. The Lord will see you in twenty minutes. Until then I am instructed to make you comfortable."

"That's three honorifics and one job title," Jaina said. "I'm only one of those things, Sergeant."

"Sergeant Major," the young man corrected, very politely. "Sergeant Major Ralle Eskin, First of the Hexani First, ma'am. And I was given the four titles by name. I'm not in the habit of guessing which one you prefer."

"Knight will do."

"Knight Solo Fel. Yes ma'am."

Behind him, at parade rest, the seven other troopers had not so much as shifted weight. Jaina let her eyes travel along the line. The seventh trooper, at the far end of the arc, was carrying something different from the rest. He had the same shielded armour and the same canvas webbing, but where the others wore a slung carbine he wore, across his back, a long pack with a low antenna projecting from the top of it. The antenna was matte black. It did not look like a communications array, and it did not look like a sensor mast. It looked like nothing she had ever seen.

It was also the source of the off-key chord she had been feeling since they exited hyperspace.

She had to make a small conscious effort not to reach for the Force to confirm it. The effort itself was instructive. The thing had only been close for a minute and she was already adjusting around it the way a person with a sprained ankle adjusted around the ankle.

She looked at Corran. Corran was looking at the trooper. Corran's mouth was very thin.

"Sergeant Major," Jaina said. "What is that one carrying?"

Eskin did not turn his head. "That's Trooper Vence, ma'am."

"That isn't what I asked."

"I know, ma'am."

A beat of quiet. Jaina felt, more than saw, Jag take a half-step closer to her elbow. T'oni's fur lifted along the line of her shoulders.

"It's a null array," Jaina said, mostly to herself. "Master. Yes?"

"Yes," Corran said.

Eskin looked, for the first time, faintly uncomfortable. He was, Jaina realised, very young and had been told to behave as though everything that was about to happen had been planned by his Lord six months in advance. He was managing it. He was not enjoying it.

"My instructions are that Trooper Vence does not power up the array unless he is given a direct verbal order from a section officer," Eskin said. "He has not been given that order. The array is cold."

"It's not cold," Jaina said.

"Pardon, ma'am?"

"It's not cold. I can feel it. So can Master Horn."

Eskin's jaw set. "Ma'am, with respect — the array is not cold. The array is in stand-by. Stand-by means it's ticking over. If I asked Trooper Vence to make it cold he'd have to crack the casing and pull the power coupling and we'd be standing in this wind for twenty minutes. The Lord asked me to keep you comfortable. I will be honest with you, ma'am. He did not ask me to make you happy."

He met her eyes.

He was, she decided, going to do well in this army.

"Thank you for the honesty, Sergeant Major."

"Ma'am."

He stepped back, replaced the helmet, and gestured to a low covered walkway leading off the apron toward the windowless grey buildings. "If you'll follow me. The Lord regrets that he hasn't sent his aide in person; the aide is dealing with something this morning. He hopes the courtesy will be accepted."

"What is the aide dealing with," T'oni asked.

"I don't know, Senator. It's above my pay grade."

"Take a guess."

A flicker of a smile under the helmet's visor. Eskin was, after all, very young.

"Something he wants to murder, Senator. I couldn't tell you which thing."

He turned smartly on his heel and began to walk. The arc of troopers broke into a column on either side. Jaina fell in between Jag and Corran. T'oni took the rear, and her small dusty-cream head moved constantly, taking in the apron, the hangars, the long blunt shape of a freighter parked at the edge of the field with its loading ramp down and a forklift trundling pallets up the slope.

The pallets were stencilled with the six-hexagon mark. Beneath the mark was a stencilled number and a stencilled word, and the stencilled word was *MEDICAL*.

T'oni tipped her head. Jaina caught the look.

"He feeds them," T'oni murmured, just loud enough for Jaina to hear. "And he heals them. He always has."

"I know."

"That's the part I find hardest to forgive."

Jaina said nothing.

They walked.

The covered walkway was duracrete, ribbed by a low corrugated roof, the kind of structure thrown up in two days by an engineer who knew his materials and didn't care what they looked like. Beyond it lay an open courtyard between hangars, and in the courtyard a squad of troopers were drilling without weapons, moving through a series of crisp paired stances that Jaina did not recognise. They were not Imperial close-combat doctrine. They were not Echani. They were something else. A non-commissioned officer paced behind them shouting cadence in a flat hard accent — not Coruscanti, not Core at all, something with the long vowels of the Outer Rim. The troopers' feet hit the duracrete in unison on each cadence beat. Their helmets were stacked in a precise pyramid against the wall behind them. They were sweating in the cold.

A pair of repulsor sleds passed the walkway going the other way, low and humming, each carrying a tarpaulin-covered shape that might have been a vehicle subassembly or might have been a piece of artillery; Jaina could not tell and chose not to stare. Two technicians in coveralls walked alongside the sleds, neither armed, both with toolbelts. Civilian techs, on a military base, walking unescorted. She filed it.

"Sergeant Major," Jag said, conversationally. "How many troopers does the Lord have on station here at the capital?"

"I'm not allowed to answer that question, sir."

"Of course."

"Sir."

"How many troopers does the Lord have under arms in total?"

"I'm not allowed to answer that either, sir."

"Could you tell us how many he had a year ago?"

Eskin did not turn his head. The flicker of his cheek, just visible under the line of the helmet, suggested he was enjoying himself. "I'd have to ask Section One, sir. I imagine they would tell you to estimate it yourself from the public payroll declarations."

"There are public payroll declarations?"

"Yes, sir. Section Three publishes them every quarter. They're on the holonet."

"They are not on the holonet," T'oni said behind them, calmly.

"They are on our holonet, Senator," Eskin said. "I'll have a copy of the most recent one sent to your quarters."

The walkway turned at a corner, and the corner opened onto a wider corridor with a duracrete floor sealed under a black resin and walls painted the same dull grey-green that had been applied to the troopers' armour. There were no portraits on the walls. There were no banners. At one bend a recruiting poster had been pasted up, edge to edge with a maintenance schedule. The recruiting poster showed a young woman in the new scrimmed armour holding her helmet against her hip, and beneath her in plain black letters were three sentences:

*WE FEED YOU. WE PAY YOU. WE BRING YOU HOME.*

Jaina read it twice.

"That's not what they usually promise," she said.

"No, ma'am," Eskin said.

"Do you do all three?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"All three?"

"All three, ma'am."

She thought about that for the next forty paces. Behind her she could feel T'oni thinking about it too.

They passed through two security checkpoints. At the first, a young woman with a datapad confirmed that Sergeant Major Eskin had a delegation of four, looked up at them one at a time, nodded, and waved them through without asking for credentials. At the second, a corporal asked Master Horn very politely to set his lightsaber on a scanning tray. Corran did. The corporal looked at the tray, said *Thank you, Master Jedi*, and handed the weapon back. He did not ask for Jaina's. He had, she realised after a few steps, asked for Corran's because Corran was older, not because Corran was a more dangerous Jedi. The Hexani Confederation had its own intelligence on her. It had a section devoted to her.

The realisation should have been insulting. She did not have time to be insulted by it. The corridor was opening onto a wide square room with no furniture in it and three doors on the far wall, and Sergeant Major Eskin had turned to face the delegation and put his heels together.

"This is the holding chamber, ma'am, sirs. The Lord is finishing a meeting through the centre door. He'll come for you when he's ready. There is water, kaf, and tea on the side console. The tea is good. I'd recommend it."

"Where do you grow tea on Hexani V?" T'oni asked.

"We don't, Senator. The Lord imports it."

"From?"

Eskin almost smiled. "I couldn't tell you, Senator."

He turned, walked back the way they had come, and the door at the entrance to the chamber sealed behind him with a small dry kiss of pressurised air. The four of them were alone.

For a long moment nobody spoke.

Jag walked to the side console, lifted the lid of the tea urn, sniffed, and lowered it. "It is good tea," he said.

"Of course it is," Jaina said.

"Sit," Corran said. "All of you. He's going to keep us in here at least fifteen minutes."

"How do you know?"

"Because he's a junior officer pretending to be a head of state and a junior officer always makes you wait."

T'oni made the small considering sound again. She had not made it for some time. "It might also be," she said, "that he is genuinely busy."

"It might," Corran agreed. "It's not exclusive. Sit, Jaina."

She sat.

She did not drink the tea.

---

The door at the far wall opened thirteen minutes later, and a thin grey-skinned man Jaina did not recognise stepped through it. He was perhaps fifty, with the deep-shadowed eye sockets of a person who had not slept well in years, and he carried a datapad clutched against his chest like a hymnal. He looked at the four of them with the brisk apologetic expression of an aide who had been informed he was running late by other people who had decided to run late themselves.

"My apologies for the wait," he said. "I am Eron Vass, the Lord's chief of staff. He's nearly ready. If you would come through."

He held the door.

They came through.

The room beyond was not a throne room. It was an office.

It was a very large office, but it was an office. There were no banners. There was no carpet — the floor was the same black-sealed duracrete as the corridor outside, and somebody had laid a long, threadbare rug across the centre of it that did not match the room and had clearly been brought in for the meeting. There was a heavy desk along one wall, with three datascreens at it and a small forest of styluses in a chipped mug; there was a second desk perpendicular to it, taller, made for working standing up. There was a long sand-coloured couch against the right-hand wall that had cigar burns along one arm and a faint indentation in the centre cushion shaped like a man who had spent a lot of nights sleeping on it. There was a low brass-bound chest at the foot of the couch with a hookah on top of it. The hookah was cold but recently used; she could smell the resin in the bowl.

A conference table had been pulled into the centre of the room. Four chairs on one side, three on the other. There was a tea service on the table, two carafes of water, a plate of biscuits, and a small bowl of dried fruit. The biscuits were stacked unevenly, as though somebody had nicked one and put the plate down again in a hurry.

The man at the standing desk had his back to them.

He was not tall. He was not even what most people would have called large. He was, however, *built* — the kind of dense, packed muscularity that belonged to a person who lifted heavy weights three times a week as a religious observance. He wore a plain dark uniform tunic without insignia, the sleeves rolled to mid-forearm, the shoulders held square, and his hair was the kind of short brown crop that had not seen a barber in three weeks. There was a coffee mug in his right hand and a stylus in his left, and he was using neither.

He was reading.

He read the screen on the standing desk for another ten seconds while the delegation stood behind him. Eron Vass made no attempt to interrupt. T'oni made no attempt to speak. Jaina, who had been read at by senators and admirals and kings and her own mother, recognised the technique. It was not posturing. It was a man finishing a paragraph.

The paragraph finished. He stylused something in a margin, tapped the screen twice, dropped the stylus into the styluses jar without looking, and turned around.

He had a frown on his face.

Jaina had been told he had a frown on his face. The reports had said so. The holos had shown it. She had not understood, until she saw it in person, that the frown was not an expression. It was the shape of him. His face at rest furrowed slightly between the brows and pulled down at the corners of the mouth, as if the bones beneath the skin had been assembled by a craftsman in a bad mood. He had the rounded jawline of a man who had been heavyset as a boy and grown out of it, and a three-day stubble of the kind that meant nothing more sinister than that he had not bothered, and pale brown eyes that took in the four of them in a single sweep and did not register surprise at any of them.

"Right," he said.

His voice was a Corellian-Rim drawl, thicker around the consonants than Han's had ever been, the kind of accent Jaina had last heard out of a gunnery sergeant on a tramp freighter. It was a voice that had spent its entire life shouting up a chain of command.

"Sit down. All of you. Sit. Vass, get out, you don't need to be in here."

"My Lord, the minutes —"

"I'll dictate them after."

"You hate dictating, my Lord."

"I'll hate it later. Out."

Vass bowed his head, glanced once at the delegation in faint apology, and went out. The door sealed behind him.

The man crossed to the table in three steps, dragged the chair at the head of the long side around so that it was not quite at the head and not quite parallel, sat in it, and put his coffee mug down on the table in a place where it left a ring. He did not move the mug off the ring.

"Sit," he said again, more mildly. "I'm not going to bite anyone."

They sat. Jaina took the chair opposite him. Jag at her left, Corran at her right, T'oni at Corran's right. The man across the table looked at each of them in turn, taking just long enough on each face to suggest he was actually looking and not performing the act of looking, and then his gaze settled on Jaina.

"Princess Solo Fel."

"Knight Solo Fel," she said.

He paused. He looked at her the way she had once seen a freighter captain look at a customs droid who had asked him to itemise his cargo at three in the morning.

— *what kind of title is that*, he thought.

It came across the table loud and unguarded, as raw and inadvertent as a stubbed toe. He was not shielded against a Jedi. He was, she realised, not shielded against anything; he simply did not bother to keep his interior monologue dressed for company. The phrase had the dry deadpan of a man genuinely confused, with an undercurrent of *kriff this lady*.

She kept her face still. It was not easy.

"Knight," he repeated, in a tone that managed to convey both that he had heard her and that he had elected to file the correction under *bantha*. "Knight Solo Fel. All right. I'm Jiyad Maerko."

"Lord Maerko," T'oni offered, neutrally.

"Lord's fine. Mister's fine. Maerko's better. I answer to all of them. Don't call me Your Excellency, I'll laugh in your face, and you've all come too far for me to laugh in your face inside the first five minutes." He picked up his coffee mug, drank from it, made a face at the temperature, and put it back exactly on its ring. "Now. We're here because I asked Head of State Fel to bring me a delegation from the Alliance, and Head of State Fel brought me his wife. That's a compliment to me, I think, and I'm taking it as one. Hello, Head of State."

"Lord Maerko."

"How was the trip?"

"Uneventful."

"That's because I made it uneventful. You're welcome." He looked at Corran. "Master Horn."

"Lord Maerko."

"I read your file on the way here from the lift. The file's eighteen years out of date. We should fix that."

"I'm flattered."

"You shouldn't be. I read everybody's file. Senator T'oni."

"Lord Maerko."

"You voted against the embargo last cycle."

"I did."

"Why?"

T'oni did not flinch. "Because embargoes don't work."

"Mm." He nodded slowly, as if she had said something interesting and not something that every senator in the Alliance had been saying for two years. "All right. So. The four of you are here. We've all said our names. We've all not drunk our tea. What does the GA want?"

Jaina opened her mouth. He kept going.

"And before you answer that, Knight, I should tell you what I want, because it'll save us a few rounds."

He leaned back in the chair. The chair creaked. He was a heavy man, in a small chair, and Jaina suspected the chair had been chosen for him because every other chair in the building was too important to bring into a meeting.

"What I want," he said, "is for the Alliance to break. I want it balkanised. I want every senator on Denon to wake up in a hundred years and realise they're a city-state again. I want the Jedi Order finished. Not killed — I don't want to kill any more of you than I already have, it's bad for business — I want it *dissolved*. I want the temple shuttered and the records dispersed and your council put out to grass." He gestured at her, casually, the way a man might gesture at the weather. "What old Palpatine did. Only without the speeches."

The room was very quiet.

He let it be quiet for a count of three, and then he laughed, briefly, at his own audacity, and the laugh was the surprise. It was not a tyrant's laugh. It was the laugh of a sergeant in a mess hall who had just told a joke he knew was over the line.

"But I'm not going to get any of that," he said. "I know I'm not. That's a wish. I keep it on my fridge. What I will settle for, today, between us, is some concessions. I'd like to walk you through them, and you can tell me which ones make you choke."

Jaina did not let her face move.

"Go ahead," she said.

"Three things." He raised three fingers. "First. The Braxant Run, the Celanon Spur, and the part of the Hydian Way between Bastion and Kuat. I want unrestricted commercial transit for vessels flagged out of the Confederation. Not military transit. Commercial. I'll abide by your customs at every port. I won't run gunships down the lane. I just want my freighters not stopped." A pause. "I have a lot of freighters."

"That's a single concession," Jag said.

"It's three lanes."

"It's a single concession with a triple barrel."

"Fine, then I'm asking for three barrels and you can call them whatever you like."

He folded the first finger down. Held up the second.

"Second. Recognition. Not love. Not embassies, even, if you can't stomach embassies — I'll take consulates. I want the GA Senate to acknowledge, on the record, that the Hexani Confederation is a sovereign polity. We are. You know we are. I know you know. We're doing this anyway. Stop pretending it's an open question."

"And the third?" Jaina said.

He folded the second finger down. He held up the third for an extra beat, almost theatrical, and then he sighed and dropped the hand on the table.

"Third. Stop sending Jedi to die in my border garrisons. Stop it. I'm asking nicely. I'm not interested in your Order, I'm not threatened by your Order, I'm not collecting your Order. Every Knight I have to bury costs me a month of bad press, and your Council never seems to learn that I will, in fact, bury them. So either start sending people I can actually negotiate with, or stop sending them. Either is fine. The status quo is not."

"What about the territory?" T'oni said.

He looked at her with a flat fond patience, the look a man gave a small dog that had finally remembered the trick. "What about it?"

"You took seventeen systems."

"I did."

"The treaty assumes you give some of them back."

"The treaty assumes a lot of things." He drank from his coffee mug. "I'm not giving any of them back. Don't even — I see you opening your mouth, Senator, don't. They're mine. I took them. I'm not giving them back. Anything else is a no."

"The Confederation has been described —" T'oni began.

"Yes."

"— as having committed —"

"Yes."

"— a number of atrocities."

"A number," he agreed.

"The Alliance —"

"The Alliance," he said, "burned a hospital on Caluula in 28 ABY because it had three Yuuzhan Vong wounded in it and a sniper on the roof. I have the names of the wounded. I have the names of the children in the maternity ward two floors down from the wounded. I know the Alliance committee that decided that hospital was a tactically acceptable cost. I know which two senators chaired it. Do not, Senator, sit in my office and talk to me about atrocities. We will both end the morning angrier than we want to be."

T'oni's fur went very flat against her shoulders. She did not answer. Jaina caught Corran's eye. Corran was, very faintly, smiling.

"What about the stormtrooper programme," Jaina said.

"What about it."

"We've had requests in our committee for two years for you to wind it down."

He stared at her.

He stared at her for long enough that she could feel Jag, beside her, prepare to intervene. She put her hand under the table and squeezed his knee once. He did not intervene.

"Eat a dick, Knight," Jiyad Maerko said.

She blinked.

"I was a stormtrooper. I *am* a stormtrooper. I am Corporal TK-2208 of Besh Company, garrisoned on Hexani V under Moff Gennadius, and on the morning the Moff stuck his hand in the till and the Fel Empire stuck a warrant on his desk and Besh Company came into the armoury and started shooting their friends, I was the corporal who lived. The reason I lived is the rest of my company was a stormtrooper. So no. No, I'm not winding down the stormtrooper programme. I'm not. Move on."

The vulgarity hung in the room.

Jaina realised — very slowly, the way one realises a tone of voice in a foreign language — that she had not, in fact, been insulted. She had been told *no* by a man who was rationing his diplomatic patience for things he cared about, and the *no* had come out in the dialect he had been raised in. He had then named his service number to her, unprompted, the way a man might show a stranger his hands. She filed that too. It was a more useful piece of information than the answer.

"All right," she said.

He looked at her, and the frown reshaped itself by a fraction, and for the first time it was clearly *deliberate*. He was looking at her with curiosity.

"All right?"

"All right," she said again. "We'll come back to the stormtrooper programme. What about prisoners?"

"What about them."

"You have hundreds of Alliance personnel held in your territory."

"I have *thousands* of Alliance personnel held in my territory."

"We'd like them back."

"I'm sure you would." He picked up the coffee mug. Looked at it. Set it down again. "Slaves, you mean."

"That's a different word."

"It's a more honest one, but fine. POWs. I'm not opposed to a phased release. I'll trade for them at standard wartime rates. I don't keep them for fun, Knight, I keep them because your Alliance issued an executive directive at the start of the Trickle that said any Confederation personnel taken were to be treated as pirates and tried as pirates, and pirates we hang. So I keep mine, you keep yours, and we work out the swap city by city. Fine. I'm fine with that conversation."

"And the Jedi prisoners?"

He hesitated.

It was not a long hesitation, but it was there. She watched it happen behind his eyes. He had been answering questions like a man flipping pages, and now there was a page he had to actually read before he flipped it.

"I have one Jedi prisoner," he said. "*One*. I haven't been collecting them. The others — your others — died in the engagements they were sent into. I'll send the bodies back if you want them. I have the bodies."

"We want the bodies."

"You'll have them."

"And the one."

He looked at her.

"You'll meet the one," he said. "She'll be in shortly with the tea."

He picked up his coffee mug, drank the last of the cold coffee, set it back exactly on its ring, and called over his shoulder, without raising his voice particularly: "Vesi."

The far door opened.

The woman who came through it was tall, lean, green-skinned, marked along the cheekbones and chin with the small dark diamonds of a Mirialan adolescence completed long after most Mirialans completed theirs. She wore a plain dark tunic and plain dark trousers and soft boots and a thin band of black metal around her throat that fitted close enough to be uncomfortable without being decorative. She carried a tray. The tray had a fresh pot on it, four clean cups, and a small dish of biscuits.

She walked across the office to the conference table, set the tray down at Jiyad Maerko's elbow without looking at him, and began pouring tea into the four clean cups with a steady professional hand. Her wrists were narrow. Her shoulders were broader than they had been in the Order files, the way the shoulders of a person who had been doing physical labour for two years got broader, and her hair was pulled back in a single short braid and she did not, in any of those two minutes, look at Jaina Solo Fel.

She finished pouring. She handed cups, one at a time, to Jag, then T'oni, then Corran, then Jaina. When she reached Jaina she met Jaina's eyes for the first time. Her eyes were a flat tired grey-green and they held nothing in them at all.

"Tea, Knight," she said.

"Vesi," Jaina said quietly.

"Tea."

Jaina took the cup.

Vesi Tellan straightened, lifted the empty tray, turned, and went and stood three paces behind Jiyad Maerko's chair with the tray held in both hands at her waist, and her face composed back into the neutral stillness it had worn when she came in.

Jiyad Maerko had been watching the whole exchange without expression.

"Right," he said. "Now we can keep going."

He picked up his own cup. Looked at it. Set it back down without drinking.

"For the sake of cleanliness," he said, without turning his head, "Vesi, the cup."

"Which cup," Vesi Tellan said behind him. Her voice was very flat.

"My cup."

"Your cup's clean."

"Vesi."

"It's clean. I poured it. Drink your tea."

He closed his eyes for a count of two. He did not look angry. He looked, very briefly, like a man whose niece had answered him back at a family dinner and who was electing, for the sake of the company, not to make a scene about it. Then he opened the eyes and looked at Jaina across the table with the expression of a man who had decided to pretend the previous three seconds had not happened.

"Where were we," he said.

"Phased release," Jaina said.

"Right. Phased release. I'll come back to that. There's something in front of it I need to clear off the table first."

He took a breath. He let it out slowly.

He was, Jaina realised — with a small cold corner of her attention that was not particularly Jedi and not particularly diplomatic but was, in fact, very much her father's daughter — looking forward to the next part of the conversation.

So, in a different way, was she.

She set the tea down without tasting it, and she folded her hands on the table, and she waited for the man across from her to begin.
