# Chapter 1 — The Corporal at the Top of the Tower

## Part 1 of 3

---

The shuttle came down through Hexani V's upper atmosphere the way an old freighter comes down anywhere — heavily, with the cabin lights flickering twice when the repulsors caught, and the deckplates ticking under the boots of people who pretended not to notice.

Jaina Solo Fel had logged the tick. She had logged the flicker, the slight overdraw on the starboard side, and the smell coming up through the floor vents, which was the smell of cheap coolant and somebody's lunch. The shuttle was Imperial work, but old Imperial work, the kind that pre-dated the Fel reforms and most of the protocols that came with them. Somewhere along the way the ship had been bolted back together by hands that did not feel sentimental about manufacturers.

She let the breath out she had been holding since orbit. The Force gave her the cabin the way it had always given her cabins — Jag's careful steadiness on her left, Senator T'oni's quieter, more guarded mind behind her, Kyp Durron's like a banked coal-fire across the aisle. There was nothing wrong in here. The wrongness was outside.

"You feel it?" she said, not looking over.

"I feel my back teeth," Kyp said.

"That's the array."

"That's what I said."

Jag, on her left, leaned forward and pressed two gloved fingers against the viewport. He watched the city come up beneath them with the same unblinking patience he had used a hundred times on a hundred different worlds. He did not speak yet. He was counting.

The city below was not what their briefing had said it would be.

Their briefing had said: *Hexani V, Outer Rim, Imperial mining colony, tibanna and trace-metal extraction, population estimate one-point-four million, capital a converted refinery township.*

The capital below was not a converted refinery township anymore. It had been a refinery township once — Jaina could see the bones of it, the cracking-towers ringed in old durasteel, the sulfur drifts where the leeward slopes had been bleached the color of bone — but somebody had laid new streets across the bones, and put new roofs over the new streets, and run a tram line out to a new plant on the eastern flats that was unmistakably a foundry, and was not finished, and was already working.

"That brief," Jag said quietly, "was lazy."

"Yeah."

"How many cranes."

"I stopped counting at thirty."

"Mm."

He sat back. He did not say anything else about the brief. He did not need to. The brief had said one-point-four million and Jaina could see two cities from the viewport already.

The shuttle banked. The landing complex came around — a wide black apron set into the side of a mesa, ringed by anti-air emplacements that did not bother to hide themselves, three pairs of TIE Vanguards parked on the deck below with their canopies cracked and their pilots leaning on the wings smoking. Past the apron, in the second ring, the troopers were already in formation.

The white was different.

Jaina noticed that first, and then she noticed the rest of it.

The armor was white the way Imperial armor was always white, but the gloss was off — newer plate, a matte finish, less reflection — and the silhouettes were wrong. There were hard angular packs on every back, low between the shoulder blades, and short antennae at the collar, and small blue indicator lights set under the chin guards. Every single trooper. Not one without.

"Shields," Jag said.

"Personal shields. Every man." Kyp's voice had gone flat. "Standard issue. That's not standard issue anywhere."

"It is here," Jaina said.

The shuttle settled. The repulsors sighed down. The ramp hydraulics started their slow, low-pressure exhale, and the four of them stood up.

Senator G'Mai T'oni rose last. She was a slight woman in her fifties, dark-skinned, dressed in the soft greys of the Alliance diplomatic corps, with a single platinum pin on her collar that meant she could speak for Cal Omas's office in a room and bind it. She had not spoken since they had cleared the atmosphere. She did not speak now. She tugged her sleeves down past her wrists, the way she did before she walked into anywhere she did not control, and gave Jaina the smallest possible nod.

*Your read,* the nod said. *I'll take it from there.*

Jaina nodded back.

The ramp opened.

The first thing she felt was the heat. Hexani V's primary was a fat yellow K-type, and the air on the apron had the dry rolling weight of a desert summer evening, full of grit and metal. The second thing she felt was the Force going thin.

Not gone. Thin.

She had felt this before, on training exercises with ysalamiri cages, and she had felt it during the war when the Vong's grafted shadows had eaten her sense of the room. This was neither. This was someone reaching into the back of her own head and turning a small dial half a click to the left, and the result was that the troopers waiting at the foot of the ramp had no weight in the Force at all, and the apron behind them had too much, and somewhere off to her right a power conduit was singing to her like it was alive.

Kyp's hand brushed hers in passing. *Don't trust it.*

She didn't have to be told.

The trooper at the foot of the ramp lifted his helmet off. He was a young man with a sergeant's tab on his collar and a thin red sunburn across the bridge of his nose.

"Master Solo Fel." He had a thick rim-world drawl. He pronounced Fel like he was holding it at arm's length. "Colonel Fel. Senator T'oni. Master Durron. Welcome to Hexani Five. If you'll come with me, please."

He did not put his helmet back on. He carried it in the crook of his arm and turned smartly and started walking, and the squad of ten behind him moved without a word, two on point, four on flank, four trailing. The flankers' carbines were not E-11s. The barrels were shorter, the receivers heavier, and the magazines sat behind the trigger group, not under the foregrip. The stocks had been folded.

And one trooper, the third in the trailing four, carried something else entirely.

It was a long matte tube, slung over the shoulder, with a cooling fin assembly running its length and a smaller blunt projector mounted under the muzzle. There were three small lights along its side, two amber, one green. The thing whined, low and steady, just at the edge of hearing, and the Force around it went thin the way the air went thin behind a turbine. Jaina did not have to look at Kyp to know he had seen it.

*That's the null array,* she thought.

She felt Kyp's shoulder come up, half an inch, the way it always did when he was angry and trying not to be.

The sergeant did not turn around. "Through the inner doors, please, sirs. Lord Maerko's expecting you."

Jag's eyes had not stopped moving since the ramp came down. He cataloged the apron without making it obvious he was cataloging the apron. Jaina knew the look. He was filing the gas-vent placement, and the second-tier sentries on the catwalks above, and the fact that one of the parked TIEs had a paint scheme that was not Imperial Remnant standard and not Fel and not anything she had seen.

She put her hand under his elbow for a moment as they walked. Not a steadying. A reminder. *You're here as my husband. You're here as the Empire's representative second.* The reminder she had given him three times in the last day, because Jag's instincts in any military complex were not to be a representative of anything except the chain of command in the room.

His mouth moved slightly. Not a smile. *Yes. I know.*

The inner doors were a pair of heavy blast hatches with the Hexani sigil painted across them in matte black — a six-rayed asterisk over a single horizontal bar. Below the sigil, in small, neat Aurebesh stencil: *NO WEAPONS BEYOND THIS POINT EXCEPT BY INVITATION.*

The sergeant stopped at the doors.

"Master Solo Fel. Master Durron." He turned and faced them and held the helmet under his arm. He had bad teeth and good eyes and he was being polite the way troopers were polite, which was the politeness of someone who had been told to be. "Your sabers stay on your belts. Lord Maerko was very specific."

"Was he," Kyp said.

"He says, quote, *I'm not stupid enough to take a Jedi's saber off her,* unquote. He says, quote, *they keep them. They don't draw them.* End quote. Sirs." The sergeant's expression did not change.

Jaina almost smiled.

"Understood, Sergeant."

"Thank you, Master."

The blast doors opened.

---

The corridor beyond was nothing like an Imperial corridor.

Imperial corridors were grey, cold, lit from above with a flat panel that turned every uniform white into a uniform corpse. This corridor was warm-toned, the walls a deep iron oxide, the lighting recessed into shoulder-height strips that gave it the feel of an evening hallway in someone's house. The deck was rubberized matting, not durasteel. The matting bore the dark scuffs of a thousand boots that had walked it carrying loads.

It smelled, very faintly, of caf.

"Through here, please."

The sergeant led them down the corridor at a steady pace. Two of his troopers had stayed at the blast doors. Two had come inside. The remaining four were waiting at the next junction, and stepped in behind the senator without being told. They moved like men who had walked this hallway a thousand times in formation and a thousand more in their sleep.

Jaina counted side doors as they walked.

Six on the left. Eight on the right. All closed. All keyed with a small mag-pad above the handle. Some of the pads were lit blue. Two were lit red. One was lit yellow, which Jaina did not know the meaning of.

A junior officer came out of one of the blue doors carrying a flimsi tablet, saw them, stopped dead, snapped to attention, and did not salute. He held the tablet against his chest and pressed himself against the wall to let them pass. As Jaina went by he murmured, "Master."

She inclined her head a fraction.

He went back to walking the moment she had passed.

That, she thought. That was the thing. The salute that wasn't quite a salute. The Master that wasn't *sir.*

This was not an Imperial garrison.

It was something else.

"Senator," T'oni said, in her quiet bureaucrat's voice. She was looking up. "The vault ceiling."

The vault ceiling, twenty meters above them, was a long arched span of structural beams that had been painted over recently and not very well. Beneath the paint, in places, Jaina could make out the ghost of older lettering. The lettering was a serial number. The lettering said *KUAT DRIVE YARDS — CONTRACT 41-AAA-* and then a series of digits Jaina could not read past the paint.

Jag had seen it. He did not speak. He filed it.

"This corridor," T'oni said softly, "was a hull section."

"A ventral hull section," Jag said.

"From?"

"From a *Procurator*-class. Or a *Bellator.* Most likely the former. They didn't have anything else on the boneyards out here."

"They scrapped a battlecruiser and built a hallway out of it."

"They built more than a hallway."

T'oni did not answer. She kept walking. But she had filed it, too. They all had.

They were not being led down a hallway. They were being led through the inside of a dead Imperial warship's spine that someone had laid down on its side and roofed over and turned into the central artery of a planetary capital, and the roof and the floor and the walls were doing real work, and the wiring above them was new wiring, and the conduits below them were new conduits, and the doors set into the bones were doors that had been cut into them by someone with a torch and an opinion about where the door ought to go.

Jaina had read a thousand briefings about Imperial Remnants in the East. None of them had ever described this.

Their briefing had been lazy.

The sergeant stopped at a final set of doors. These ones were single-leaf, not double, and they were not blast doors. They were plain wood, dark, sanded, with a small brass plate above the handle bearing one Aurebesh letter: *jenth.* Just the letter.

"Lord Maerko's office, sirs."

He opened the door.

---

The room beyond was not what they expected.

It was not large. It was not small. It was an office, and it looked like an office. There was a desk along the back wall, broad, plain, made of some local dark wood and scarred along the front edge from being kicked. There were three flimsi-stacks on the desk and a half-empty caf cup and a model of a Star Destroyer that looked like it had been built by hand out of plasticine and was missing the bridge tower.

There were two long tables along the side walls, covered in scattered datapads and what looked like equipment schematics held down by mugs.

There was a low couch.

There were three potted plants of which two were obviously real and one was obviously plastic and dusty.

There were no banners. There were no portraits. There were no Imperial sigils. There was no throne. There was a thin man in a black tech-corps tunic standing at a side console pretending to be busy, and at the desk —

At the desk there was a man in shirtsleeves drinking tea.

He looked up when they came in.

He set the cup down.

He stood.

He came around the desk wiping his palm on the side of his trousers, and stuck the same palm out toward Senator T'oni first, which was correct, and shook it once, firmly, and let go, and said:

"Senator. Welcome to the Confederation. I appreciate you coming all the way out here. Colonel Fel." A shorter, more military nod for Jag, but the hand offered the same way. "Master Solo Fel. Master Durron."

Jaina watched him.

She had read his file twice on the way out. The file had a holo. The holo had been taken when he was nineteen and wearing TK-armor at a graduation parade, and it had shown a tall thin boy with a recruit's haircut and the empty face of a man whose chin strap was too tight.

The man in front of her was not tall, was not thin, was not a boy.

He was perhaps a meter and seventy-five. He was built like a stevedore — broad at the shoulder, thick in the forearm, no excess at the waist, the kind of frame that meant somebody lifted heavy things four mornings a week and ate well. He was not handsome. He was not ugly. His face was the face of a man you would not turn your head to look at in a transit station. It was set in a permanent, mild frown — the look of a man who had bitten into a piece of fruit and was not yet sure whether it was off.

She caught the surface of him in the Force, briefly, before the array out in the hall ate it.

He felt like a wall.

Not the dark side. Not the light. A wall, the way a strong-willed non-Force-sensitive felt — settled, occupied, and entirely uninterested in being read.

"Sit down," he said. "Please. All of you. You want tea? I've got tea. I've got caf if you want caf. It's bad caf, fair warning, the local stuff's about half chicory. The tea is decent. Brought up from Tunjin." He looked past them and lifted his chin at the man at the console. "Karry, four cups. Hot water. The good leaves, not the floor sweepings."

The technician — Karry — said "Yes, Lord," and went out a side door.

Maerko sat back down.

He did not sit on the desk. He did not lean. He sat in his chair like a man who had been on his feet too long that morning and was looking forward to taking the load off. He pushed a flimsi-stack aside without looking at it.

"Right," he said. "What does the Alliance want."

It was not a polite question. It was not a hostile question. It was the question of a man who had a lot of meetings that day and would prefer to get this one over.

T'oni took a chair. She arranged her sleeves. She crossed her ankles. Jaina had seen her open negotiations with the Verpine and the Bothans and once a delegation of Kuati nobles, and she opened them all the same way — slowly, and with the patience of a woman who had outlasted six Chiefs of State.

"The Alliance," T'oni said, "would like to understand what you intend to do."

"Intend to do where."

"Anywhere."

He looked at her. He considered.

"All right," he said. "I'll tell you. I intend to keep what I've taken. I intend to take a couple more things. I intend to develop what I've got. I intend not to be invaded. That's about the whole of it."

"That is a great deal."

"It's a list of four."

T'oni allowed a very small smile.

"Lord Maerko, the systems you've — *taken* — include four signatories of the Alliance Charter."

"Three."

"Three?"

"Tilarn withdrew their signature in '38. You should update your files."

T'oni did not blink. She filed it. "Three signatories of the Charter. And one protectorate of the Fel Empire."

"Two."

She looked at him.

He looked back.

"The Hapan trade office on Yarkona," he said, "was a protectorate of the Fel Empire on paper. It was a smuggling clearinghouse in practice. The colonel can confirm that if he likes." A flick of his eyes at Jag. "I don't expect him to confirm it in front of you."

Jag's face did not move. Jaina was almost certain he had filed *that* in the same file as the brief.

"Lord Maerko," T'oni said, "the Alliance does not accept your characterization of these annexations."

"The Alliance doesn't have to. Senator. With respect." He picked his cup up again. He took a sip. He set it back down. "You aren't here to accept my characterization. You're here because three months ago we put down the Eighth Fleet at Tjorn and a year before that we put your Jedi strike teams in body bags and you finally figured out you can't take this sector back with what you've got. So you sent a senator and a princess and a colonel and a Master to come and ask me what I want." He tipped a hand at her. "I respect that. That's grown-up work. Most people would've kept throwing dead ones at me. So thank you. What do you want."

There was a small silence.

Kyp, who had not yet sat down, sat down.

Jaina did not speak yet. The Force around Maerko was still a wall. He was not lying. He was not performing. He was telling the truth as a man tells the truth across a cantina table — bluntly, without varnish, and with the assumption that the listener would prefer not to hear it twice.

She had met warlords before. She had not met this.

"We want," T'oni said, "an armistice. A binding armistice. With third-party verification. Across all currently engaged fronts. The Alliance and the Fel Empire are prepared to recognize the de facto authority of the Hexani Confederation within seven systems for an initial term of twelve years."

"Seventeen."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Seventeen systems. Not seven. You've got the count wrong in your brief. Again." He looked at her. He looked at Jaina. He looked at Jag. "Are all your briefs this bad."

T'oni did not answer.

"Seventeen," he said. "I can name them. You want me to name them."

"I am aware of your claims."

"They're not claims. I run them. Hospitals are open. Trams are open. The schools are open. Taxes are paid into Hexani treasury, not Coruscant treasury and not Bastion treasury. You can call them claims. I call them seventeen ledger entries on my Quartermaster-Legate's desk. You want to talk armistice? Talk it on seventeen."

"That is not a position the Alliance can accept."

"It's the position you'll accept eventually. You can do it now or you can do it in two years after I've taken the Salin Corridor off you." He sat forward. He laced his fingers. "Senator. Look. Let me lay it out. I'm going to be honest because we don't have all night and I'm told you've got dignity. Here's what I want."

He lifted one thick finger.

"The Hydian Way. The eastern segment, from Atrisia rimward to the Nyarikan Junction. Not the whole lane. The segment. I don't care what flag flies on the rest of it. I want passage rights, I want to set tariff floors at the Junction, I want my hulls bonded to transit clean."

A second finger.

"The Braxant Run. The whole run. From Yaga Minor out. The Remnant doesn't use it anymore. The Fel Empire pretends to but doesn't garrison it past the third waystation. I do garrison it. I want the title to match the practice."

A third finger.

"The Salin Corridor. Or a piece of it. We can negotiate that one. I'll be reasonable about Salin because Salin matters to your shipping more than mine and I'm not interested in starving Bothawui."

He set his hand flat.

"That's the lanes. That's not negotiable as a category. The specific segments are negotiable as a price. Second item. I keep my territory. Every system. No partition. No referenda. No third-party administration of any of the seventeen worlds. That is also not negotiable. Third item — *third* — the auxiliary program continues. I've heard the speech about the auxiliary program. I'm not interested in the speech about the auxiliary program. They were pirates, slavers, and Hutt enforcers. The hospitals on the worlds they used to raid are open. Fourth — the stormtrooper corps stays. Fifth — I will not extradite any Imperial loyalist who came to me after the Gennadius mess. Sixth — and this one'll save us all an afternoon — I'm not interested in joining the Alliance, the Fel Empire, the Imperial Remnant proper, the Jedi Council, the Bothan Council, or any other club. I'm running my sectors. You can come trade. You can come visit. You can build an embassy. Bring a senator. Bring two."

He looked at T'oni.

"That's me. In exchange. You get peace. You get the Hexani Confederation off the Tjorn front, off the Yarkona front, off the Vaszell front. You get my word that I do not develop and do not deploy strategic-grade null arrays beyond my recognized borders. You get tariff parity on Hydian. You get my trade lanes open to your hulls under flag, which —" he glanced at Jag — "Colonel, your kit-buyers will appreciate. I sell raw tibanna for less than Yag'Dhul does, and I am not in the habit of charging the Fel Empire war-rate."

He picked up his tea.

He drank.

"That's the deal."

T'oni said nothing. She was a master of saying nothing. She let the silence sit on the table like a heavy parcel, and waited to see what Maerko did.

Maerko set his cup down and looked at Jaina.

"You haven't said anything yet, Master."

"No."

"You want to."

"I'm listening."

"To what?"

"To you."

He smiled a little. Not a warm smile. A working man's smile, the smile of a man who knew a card had been played on him and was deciding whether to take the trick or fold.

"What does the Force tell you, Master Solo Fel?"

"Nothing I'll repeat in this room."

"Hah. Fair."

He sat back. He looked at her a long moment. The frown did not leave his face but the eyes behind it changed — went slightly amused, slightly considering, the way a man's eyes change when he has met someone and decided he can do business with them and is now waiting to find out if they have decided the same.

"All right," he said. "We'll come back to you. Colonel."

"Lord Maerko."

"Your father's well."

"He is."

"Good. Tell him the foundry on Yarkona's running. He'll know what I mean."

Jag's face did not change. "I will."

"He won't like it."

"He will not."

"Mm."

Maerko picked up a flimsi from the stack. He glanced at it. He set it down. Then, without changing tone, without dropping his eyes, the way a man might say *pass the salt* —

"Bring Siri in."

Karry, who had returned with a tray of tea, paused with the tray half-set down, recovered, set it down properly, said "Yes, Lord," and went out the side door again.

T'oni's eyes flicked to Jaina. Jaina's eyes did not move. She had read the brief about the captive. She had read it three times. The brief had used the words *believed killed in action at Vaszell*, and then in a footnote, *intelligence as of seventy-one days ago suggests possible captivity.* The brief had been lazy about that, too.

Kyp's stillness next to her had become the stillness of a stone before it falls.

The door opened.

The woman who came in was perhaps twenty-six. She was small, dark-haired, dressed in a plain grey tunic and plain grey trousers, soft slippers, no shoes. There was a flat band of dull bronze metal around her throat — not large, not ornamented, fitted close and seamless, with one small black diode set into the front of it. Her wrists were not bound. Her hands were not bound.

She was holding a teapot.

She did not look at Maerko. She looked at Jaina, and then at Kyp, and then at Jag, and then at T'oni. Her eyes were dry, and bright, and entirely lucid, and the Force around her was the strangest thing Jaina had felt since the apron — present, real, alive, and twisted, the way water is twisted around a stone.

"This is Siri Voss," Maerko said. He had not stood up. He was using the same voice with which he had named the Hydian Way. "Knight Voss. Captured at Vaszell. Currently in my household."

Siri Voss inclined her head very slightly. Not a bow. An acknowledgment.

"Masters," she said. Her voice was clear. It was not a slave's voice. It was the voice of a Jedi who had been a knight when she was nineteen years old and had not yet forgotten the inflection. "Colonel. Senator."

"Knight Voss," Kyp said.

The word he used was *knight.*

She heard it. Her eyes met his. She inclined her head a hair further.

"Master Durron."

Maerko watched the exchange the way a man watches a hand of sabacc he has not been dealt into.

"Pour, please, Siri."

She poured. The tea steamed. She moved around the low table with the unselfconscious grace of someone who had done this for months and resented it precisely the right amount — visible, but not large enough to spill. She set a cup in front of T'oni. She set a cup in front of Jaina. She set a cup in front of Jag. She set a cup in front of Kyp.

Kyp did not pick his up.

Siri set the pot down on a side table.

"Thank you," Maerko said.

"My pleasure, Lord," she said, and her tone said the opposite, and his mouth twitched, and she sat — without being told to sit — on a low stool behind his right shoulder, folding her hands in her lap, looking at the floor.

Jaina took her tea. Her hand was steady. Her hand was always steady.

"Lord Maerko." Senator T'oni's voice had cooled by a degree. "The detention of an Alliance citizen, a Jedi Knight, in a — collar — is —"

"Was she killed in your records?"

"That is not relevant to —"

"It's relevant to me. Was she killed in your records, Senator. Yes or no."

A small pause.

"Knight Voss was listed as missing-presumed-killed, yes."

"Right. So I didn't detain an Alliance citizen. I picked up a body that wasn't a body and I didn't shoot her, which I could have, and I didn't ransom her, which I could have, and I put a collar on her, which I'm not going to apologize for, and now she pours tea. We can call her a prisoner of war. I'm happy to call her a prisoner of war. The Alliance doesn't recognize my state, so we have a definitional problem about prisoners of war, but I'm willing to have that conversation with whoever you send to have it. Until we have it, the collar stays."

"That is —"

"Senator. Save the speech. I've heard it. I will trade her for one system."

T'oni's eyes did not move. "One system."

"One. Your pick. Off the list of seventeen. Pick a little one. I'll let you have Tilarn, actually, since Tilarn withdrew their signature and we agree that nobody owns it on paper, and you can have her, and her collar comes off in the shuttle on the way home, and we're all friends. Or you can leave her here and we can talk about the lanes. Pick."

T'oni did not pick. She did not answer.

Kyp's mug had not moved. Jaina set hers down again, untouched.

She felt Siri Voss in the Force.

Not as a prisoner. As a person. There was the wall of the collar — closer to her, brighter and tighter than the wall in the corridor — and there was something behind the wall, something that was alive and watchful and almost amused, and there was a thread of something else under it that Jaina did not understand and did not, here, in this room, in front of Maerko, want to look at closely.

The Force in her, twisted, around the collar, was not *failing.* It was being *redirected.* The collar was not stopping Siri Voss from using the Force. The collar was *interpreting* the Force she used.

Jaina was not sure if anyone else in the room had felt it.

She looked at Kyp.

His jaw was set the way it got when he was making a decision he was going to have to live with.

He looked at Siri. He looked at Jaina. He looked back at the cup of tea in front of him.

He did not pick it up.

*That,* his stillness said to her. *That is my answer about her. Not now. Not yet.*

Jaina let the breath out.

Maerko had been watching them, evenly, drinking his tea.

"Senator," he said, "while you're thinking, I want to be clear about one other thing. I don't like the Sith. I don't like Jedi. I'm not particularly fond of either of you sitting on my couch, with respect. What I want, ultimately, is for the Alliance to break up the way she's already trying to break up, balkanize the way she's already balkanizing, and for the Order —" he tipped his cup toward Kyp without looking at him — "to go the way old man Palpatine sent it. That's what would please me. I'm telling you because you asked. You did ask."

"That," T'oni said quietly, "is candor."

"I'll settle for less," he said. "I'll settle for a lot less. I'll settle for the lanes and being left alone. I'm not a fanatic, Senator. I'm a man with a sector to run. But you asked what I want, so I'm telling you what I want, and you can take it back to Cal Omas as my opening position on the philosophy of the thing. He'll appreciate the candor too. He's that kind of man."

"He is."

"I always liked him. I shot at his troops, but I always liked him."

Jaina, very carefully, did not look at Siri Voss.

Siri Voss, very carefully, did not look at Jaina.

The Force between them moved, gently, like a fingertip drawn through still water.

*Later,* it said.

Maerko glanced at her then — at Jaina — and the frown shifted a fraction, the way a man's frown shifts when he is working out a sum he has not been given all the parts of. She felt the surface of his thought brush hers — not because he had reached for her, because he hadn't, but because the wall around him had a single small window where he was looking out of it at her, and the window happened to be open.

*Master,* his thought said, drily. *What kind of title is that. Pick a rank or pick a priesthood. Don't do both.*

He did not know she had heard it.

She let it pass without a flicker. She had been letting things like that pass since she was twelve.

From the stool behind his right shoulder Siri Voss said, mildly, to the floor:

"It's a rank, Lord. It's been a rank for nine thousand years."

Maerko's head snapped around.

"I told you," he said, "to stop —"

"I'm sitting in the room. Lord. With respect. I haven't moved. I am the tea girl. The tea girl can hear."

"The tea girl is not invited to —"

"You said *what kind of title is that.*"

"I did *not* say it."

"You thought it loud enough to —"

"I did not — I —" He stopped. He had gotten his tongue tangled around the negation. He started again and the second try was no better. "I — that is not a — Knight Voss, I did not — *say.*"

Siri Voss, looking at the floor, said tonelessly: "I — that is not a — Knight Voss, I did not — *say.*"

It was not a cruel imitation. It was a perfect one. The cadence was Maerko's, down to the small flat hitch he put on the *say*, and she did it without raising her eyes.

Jaina felt the smile come up under her ribs before she could field it. She got it back down before it reached her mouth, but it had been very close, and she suspected Senator T'oni, who was the most disciplined face in the room, had caught the start of it.

Maerko stared at the back of Siri Voss's head as if he were considering several actions and discarding them one by one because they were all undignified.

He turned. He looked at Karry. Karry had set down the empty teapot and was holding the tray flat against his chest and pressing his lips together so hard the corners had gone white. Karry was bald. Karry had been bald, Jaina would later learn, since he was nineteen. Karry's lip discipline failed by about a quarter of a millimeter, and a single hiss of air came out of his nose.

Maerko pointed at him.

"You," he said. "You are not laughing. You are not even *a person*. You are a *testicle*."

Karry made a sound.

Jag, who had finally raised his teacup to his lips, was struck mid-sip by a small involuntary spasm of the diaphragm. The tea did not, technically, leave his mouth. A fine spray of it left his nose. He brought up a gloved hand fast, but not fast enough. He set the cup down. He looked, for a very brief instant, as if he wished he were elsewhere.

Maerko, gravely, handed him a folded cloth napkin from the side of the desk.

"Colonel."

"Lord Maerko."

"It's bad caf."

"It's tea."

"It's bad tea. Different problem. Karry, *out.*"

"Lord," said Karry, and went out, and only when the door closed did Jaina hear the laugh come up through the wall, muffled and helpless, lasting nearly four seconds before it cut off.

Maerko, with great dignity, took a swallow of his own tea.

He looked at Jaina.

"Master Solo Fel," he said, "for the record, I am not normally —"

"No, Lord Maerko. Of course not."

"Mm."

He set the cup down.

The Force around him had not changed. He was a wall. But somewhere inside the wall, Jaina could feel, very faintly, a man enjoying himself — and not in the cruel way she had been bracing for since the brief. In the way a man enjoyed himself when his life was hard and his desk was full and a sharp-tongued Jedi was sitting at his right elbow telling him exactly what she thought of him in front of the people he was supposed to be threatening.

It was the most disturbing thing she had felt about him so far.

---

The aide came in then.

He was a thin man in a Section II tunic — Intelligence, Jaina noted, the directorate under Boros — and he did not knock. He crossed the room directly to Maerko's chair, bent at the waist, and murmured something into his ear.

Maerko's frown deepened.

Then it deepened further.

Then he closed his eyes for a moment.

"You're joking," he said.

The aide said something else.

"No, I heard you. I said *you're joking.* That was a sentence with meaning."

The aide held out a datapad.

Maerko took the datapad. He looked at it. He scrolled. He scrolled again. He set the datapad on the desk with the gentleness of a man who had been advised by his medics to stop breaking equipment. He pinched the bridge of his nose. He exhaled.

"All right," he said. "All right. Excuse me a moment, Senator. Family business."

He turned in his chair. He motioned the aide closer.

He lowered his voice.

He did not lower it enough.

Jaina did not lean. She did not have to lean. The Force could be made to push, and it could be made to pull, and any Jedi who had survived to her age had learned what it could be made to do at the edge of a corridor. The collar around Siri Voss's throat, twisting the Force, *redirected* it — but the redirection moved both ways, and the air in this room had been told, by its host, that it was being listened to by Jedi, and the room knew, and the room was generous.

She caught fragments.

"— hit the depot at Korreg —"

"— civilians taken, yes, Lord —"

"— the prototype hulls. They got the schematics —"

"— *prototype hulls* —"

"— Lord, the new line. Yes —"

A small pause.

"— Hark Venn —"

"— that's the working ID. Confirmed by two —"

Another pause. Longer. Then Maerko's voice, very quiet, very even:

"Put a team together. Sel's team. I want him caught alive. I want him brought here. I want — yeah. Cut his nutsack off and feed it to him. Make sure they're recording the whole thing. I wanna hear him scream when they do it. Bring me the recording. The civilians come back to their families before the recording is made, do you understand me? *Before.* Anyone who confuses the order on that one gets fed to the same dish."

The aide said, "Yes, Lord."

"Anything else?"

"No, Lord."

"Go."

The aide bowed and left.

Maerko sat back. He breathed out. He picked up his tea, found it cold, made a face, and set it down again.

From behind his right shoulder, without looking up from her hands in her lap, Siri Voss said:

"You said you'd stop doing the testicle thing."

Maerko closed his eyes.

"I told you to stop listening in, Knight Voss."

"I'm not listening. You're shouting."

"I was *whispering.*"

"You whisper like a tibanna leak."

"Knight Voss."

"Lord Maerko."

"If I have to put the muzzle setting back up I am going to be very disappointed in our progress."

"If you put the muzzle setting back up I am going to piss in your teacup the next time it's my pour."

Jag, who had been lifting his cup to his mouth, paused. He set the cup down very carefully and did not look at Jaina, because if he had looked at Jaina he would have laughed, and Jagged Fel did not laugh in negotiations.

Maerko opened one eye. He looked at Siri. He looked at the ceiling. He looked at Senator T'oni.

"This," he said, "is what I live with."

T'oni, very gravely, said: "I see, Lord Maerko."

"She was quieter when she got here."

"I imagine she was."

"I liked her better quieter."

"Mm."

He waved a hand. "Shoo, Siri. Go fix the tea. The Senator's cup is cold. The colonel's holding his like he's afraid of it. Master Durron isn't touching his at all, which is fine. Master Solo Fel —" he glanced at Jaina, dryly — "is being polite. Go."

Siri Voss rose. She picked up the teapot. She inclined her head to the room — to Jaina, very slightly, longer than the others — and she went out the side door.

The door closed behind her.

Maerko put both hands flat on his desk.

"Where were we."

"Knight Voss," Kyp said, "is the conversation."

"Knight Voss is *a* conversation. We were on the lanes. We come back to her after the lanes."

"We don't."

"Master Durron —"

"We don't."

A small, hard silence.

Maerko studied Kyp Durron. The frown on his face did not change, but his attention sharpened — the way a man sharpens a knife not to use it but to know it is sharp, in case.

"All right," he said, finally. "We'll come back to her. Soon. After the senator and I are done. Yes?"

"After the senator and you are done," Kyp said, "I will speak."

"Good. I look forward to it."

He did not. Jaina felt the lie clearly enough. It was the cleanest thing she had felt off him all afternoon, and the most honest.

She also felt — separately, harder to read, but unmistakable — that Maerko was *interested* that Kyp had said it.

She set her teacup down.

She looked at the senator. The senator looked back.

*This is what we came for,* T'oni's look said. *Now we work.*

Jaina inclined her head a fraction.

*Now we work,* she agreed.

Outside the window of the office, far below the mesa, the foundry on the eastern flats lit its evening pour. The light came up through the dust haze in a long slow column of red.

The negotiation had not yet started.

It was about to.
